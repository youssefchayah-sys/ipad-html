How EDF/Framatome is securing its supply chain

EDF won't let any link in the nuclear chain fall under foreign ownership or go out of business. It uses three different deal structures to do this: direct purchase, buy-back, and backed rescue followed by a minority stake.

From lender to partner to shareholder

20 Jun 2024

Manoir Industries, Pîtres Lender

The Paris commercial court approved Paralloy's takeover (about €86M revenue, about 470–500 staff). The funding: €6M from Paralloy, a matching State loan, €3.5M of quasi-equity from the Normandy Region, and a €3M loan from EDF. EDF was a creditor, not an owner.

24 Feb 2026

Hachette & Driout, Saint-Dizier Partner

The Lyon court selected Paralloy's offer, made in partnership with Framatome. The foundry was renamed Firth Vickers France and placed under Manoir France, with about 170 jobs kept. Paralloy and Framatome promised the court €7M of investment and work to fill the order book. Framatome was a named partner but took no shares.

17 Jul 2026

Manoir France Shareholder

Framatome bought a minority shareholding in Manoir France, which owns both Pîtres and Saint-Dizier. The percentage, price and any board seats were not disclosed. The stated reason: securing supply chains "particularly for the EPR2 program and for the defense industry" (Bernard Ponchon).

Ownership today: Paralloy Group (majority) → Manoir France (Framatome holds a minority stake) → Firth Vickers France, the former Hachette & Driout.

Part 2

The EPR2 two-way CfD

Under a two-way CfD, EDF's project company gets a fixed amount per MWh of reference volume. The open question is who captures the value outside that fixed band, and that is the subject of the dispute with Brussels.

How it works

  • The project company sells its electricity at market price. It then settles with the State on the difference between the strike and a reference price: the annual average French day-ahead price (recital 152).
  • When the reference price is below the strike, the State pays the difference. When it is above, the company pays it back. On the reference volume (66.7 TWh/yr), revenue ≈ strike.
  • The key detail: the settlement uses the reference price, not the price EDF actually sells at. Anything EDF earns above the reference price falls outside the CfD and stays with the seller.
The basis · écart de base

The State tops up an index, not EDF's sale price

The gap between EDF's actual sale price and the public reference price stays with EDF, whether it's a gain or a loss. Traders call this gap the basis.

Revenue = actual sale price + (strike − reference price)
        = strike // guaranteed by the State
        + (actual sale price − reference price) // the basis, which the seller keeps

Worked example

Strike €100/MWh, reference price €80/MWh, so the State pays €20/MWh in every case.

EDF's choices change EDF's revenue, not the State's bill.

Why the CfD is designed this way

Commercial discipline

If the State topped up the actual sale price, EDF wouldn't care how well it sold, because the State would cover any loss.

Predictable cost for the State

The State's payment depends only on a public index that no one controls.

Reference volume, not metered output

This avoids distorting production decisions (recital 136). Producing more than the reference volume is upside for EDF; producing less is EDF's risk.

What partly limits the basis

  • The strike already deducts a projected shape value of €1–3/MWh. Nuclear output is flat, so it is valued at roughly the annual average.
  • Forward-sale adjustment: AM = (forward price − reference price) × θ × k (recital 153). It shifts part of the reference toward the forward price, so part of any forward-sale gain or loss is shared (θ between 0 and 1).
  • 60/40 clawback, which applies only if project returns exceed 7–9%, checked every 5 years.

Who captures what

CfD band · project company

Strike × reference volume, fixed. It repays the investment and the loan.

Pools 1 & 2 · EDF today

The real prize. Paris wants to protect them; Brussels wants to open them up.

Extra volume & tail · EDF

Output above the reference volume and years 41–60. Neither is contested.

Above 7–9% IRR · split

60% to the State, 40% to EDF.

Market above strike · State

The State receives the CfD payments, with no defined route back to consumers yet.

Why this matters

  • The CfD protects the investment. Pools 1 and 2 decide who controls the market. Brussels' demand is about competition, not project financing.
  • Case B (contract premium) is EDF's upside. Case D (discounting) is Brussels' worry. A State-backed asset with guaranteed revenue can afford 15-year low-carbon offers no competitor can match, and use them to lock up the largest customers (verrouillage du marché).
  • Brussels' remedy: sell through the exchange. The sale price then roughly equals the reference, the basis is roughly zero, and every supplier buys EPR2 power on the same terms.
  • For EDF, pools 1 and 2 are where the upside sits. With an IRR of about 6% at a €100 strike (own model), they are what makes the economics work. Losing them doesn't put the debt at risk, but it removes EDF's margin above its target return.
  • France's best argument: excess returns are already 60% clawed back. Counter-argument: the clawback only applies above the threshold, and a contract premium can create a structural market advantage well before that point.
Part 3 · Costs & financing

How EPR2 is costed and financed, and the road to FID

The financing has three layers. A State-guaranteed loan from the Caisse des Dépôts (CDC) covers 60% of construction and is interest-free to the project until each reactor runs. A two-way CfD fixes revenue for 40 years. EDF's equity carries the rest of the construction risk, and overruns are shared with the State above €15bn. As of early November 2026 the final investment decision (FID) has not been taken. It is still targeted for end-2026, and it depends on Brussels.

Who carries which risk

Construction · EDF first, then shared

EDF takes the first €15bn of overrun. The State takes 90% of the next €15bn.

Financing during construction · State

The State pays the interest during the build (≈ €10.5bn). This is the main reason the strike can stay near €100.

Interest rates in operation · State

The project pays a fixed 3%. The State absorbs or gains the difference as Livret A moves.

Market price · CfD

Two-way settlement on the reference price for 40 years. The basis and value pools 1–5 stay with EDF (see Part 2).

Upside · capped

Above a 7–9% nominal post-tax IRR, gains are shared 60% State / 40% EDF, checked every 5 years (recitals 90–91).

The Brussels procedure (SA.119469)

Notified to the Commission

France formally notified the aid package.

In-depth investigation opened

IP/26/744, Official Journal C/2026/2619 of 7 May 2026. The Commission accepts the market failure and the need for aid. Its doubts are about proportionality and EDF's market power.

Jul 2026

Split over how the power is sold

France proposes 70% merchant sales and 30% long-term contracts. Brussels wants more liquidity and safeguards on EDF's commercial conduct. Paris says there is no disagreement on the financing itself.

Oct 2026

Conditions emerge

According to specialised press: third-party access to EPR2 output and stronger interconnection. The volume, price and sales channel are still open.

What the model says

  • The debt is very safe and the equity is thin. A minimum DSCR of 1.66× means the CfD comfortably services the CDC loan. EDF's 5.96% IRR is below its 7.5% cost of equity, so at €100 the project destroys about €6.4bn of value for EDF.
  • The 60/40 clawback is not triggered. The IRR is well under the 7–9% threshold, so the State's upside share is theoretical in the base case.
  • This is why pools 1 and 2 in Part 2 matter. The contract premium and retail margin are what can close the 1.5-point gap between 6% and EDF's target. That is exactly the value Brussels wants to open up.
  • LCOE (€93.5) is below the strike (€100), but at a blended 4.8% WACC that reflects cheap State debt. The project works for society, and is marginal for EDF's shareholders.
  • The State's real exposure is Livret A. At 3% Livret A the cost to the State rises from about €10.5bn to about €27bn nominal. At 1.5% it falls to about €2bn, because the State then earns a margin in operation.

Simplifications to declare

  • No tax-loss carry-forward and no EDF corporate debt.
  • The 60/40 clawback is flagged, not modelled as cash.
  • The CfD is modelled as strike × reference volume, with no shape value and no forward-sale adjustment, so none of the Part 2 basis is included.
  • The State's share of any overrun is paid as a grant. The loan's partial coverage of overruns and the materiality threshold are not modelled.
  • Ranges given in brackets in the decision are confidential, so their midpoints are used. EDF's internal data would take precedence.
