The spine

Six phases, built in the only order that survives contact with a real market

Most people invert this — they start with ideas, which is why most people lose. You build temperament first, philosophy second, process third, ideas last. Each phase ends in something you write, because an unwritten thesis is just a feeling looking for confirmation. Roughly four weeks per phase; compress or extend to fit your life, but never reorder.

Your antidote. This is the book that resolves your cash tension: you don't get to forecast the recession, but you do get to read whether prices embed optimism or fear, and calibrate aggressiveness to that. Second-level thinking, in plain language.

The qualitative half you're least trained in. Fisher's 15 points and the scuttlebutt method teach you to judge a business through people and field research — not just spreadsheets.

Munger

The latticework of mental models and the discipline of inversion — "always invert." For a quant mind, this is the upgrade from being a good analyst to being a good thinker. Dense; read in pieces.

Greenwald

Bruce Greenwald

Bridges directly into your strength. The earnings-power-value and asset-value methods give you alternatives to a single DCF point estimate — a more robust way to triangulate intrinsic value.

Extract: value the assets and the no-growth earnings power first; treat growth as the speculative top layer, not the foundation.
Bogle

The Little Book of Common Sense Investing

John Bogle

Short, and the philosophical backbone of your 50% ETF core. The humility argument: most active pickers, including future-you on a bad idea, lose to a cheap global index over decades. This is why the core sleeve is permanent.

Extract: costs compound against you exactly as returns compound for you; the index is the default to beat, not the fallback.
Klarman

Margin of Safety

Seth Klarman

Out of print and expensive to buy — widely available as a PDF. Risk-first portfolio thinking from a manager who, like you, treats cash as the residue of opportunity, not a market call. Reinforces the diagnostic at the heart of your coaching.

Extract: avoiding loss is the precondition of compounding; the absence of a bargain is itself a reason to hold cash.
Taleb

Fooled by Randomness

Nassim Taleb

The inoculation against your own success. The danger after a few good calls is mistaking luck for skill and sizing up. Read this in Phase 5, when you're about to size real positions.

Extract: judge decisions by the quality of the process, not the outcome of a single draw from a noisy distribution.

The three instruments

Stocks, ETFs, bonds — what each is for, and where your edge actually lives

You asked to invest wisely across stocks, ETFs and bonds. They are not three flavours of the same thing — they play different roles. The honest news: your deepest, rarest edge is in the one most retail investors ignore. Pick a tab.

Stocks & compounders
ETFs
Bonds & credit

The alpha sleeve — where judgment is rewarded

Individual businesses are where skill, patience, and circle-of-competence are actually compensated. This is also where you can do the most damage to yourself. Everything in your library — the 7-phase framework, the compounder deep-dives — serves this sleeve.

The competence to build here: judge a business (Phase 2), value it conservatively (Phase 3), demand a margin of safety, size it with discipline (Phase 5), and hold through volatility you can stomach because you decided the drawdown tolerance in advance.

The trap for you specifically: a beautiful model feels like truth. Owning the math is not owning the business. Pair every DCF with a reverse-DCF and a written "what would make me wrong."

The 7-question gauntlet — run on every single idea

  • 1 · Describe the business in one sentence→If you can't, you don't understand it yet
  • 2 · What is the moat, how durable?→Phase 2 muscle
  • 3 · What do owner-earnings look like in 5 years?→Conservative, ranged, not a point
  • 4 · What is the margin of safety at today's price?→A number, not a feeling
  • 5 · What would have to be true for you to be wrong?→The pre-mortem
  • 6 · Would you hold if the market closed 5 years?→The temperament test
  • 7 · What is your position size, and why?→Conviction-weighted, hard-capped

The foundation sleeve — designed to need no decisions

ETFs are the humility instrument. They guarantee you participate in global growth even when your individual picks disappoint — and over 20 years, most pickers do. Your existing architecture treats the ETF core as a permanent floor that never drops below 40%. That discipline is the whole point.

The competence to build here is small but real: understand TER as a compounding drag, domicile and withholding tax (Irish-domiciled accumulating ETFs are usually optimal for an EU investor — you already have this in your master table), replication method, and CAPE as a temperature reading on expected returns rather than a timing trigger.

The discipline: the ETF sleeve is where you do nothing. If you're ever tempted to sell the core to chase an exciting single stock, that temptation is itself the signal to wait 48 hours. Activity is the enemy of compounding here.

Your under-exploited edge

Bonds and credit are where your career secretly trained you

Most retail value investors treat bonds as a black box and buy a bond ETF blindly. You spent a career in energy project finance underwriting exactly the analysis that prices a corporate bond: can this entity service its debt, under what coverage, with what seniority, against which covenants, with what recovery if it can't?

A corporate bond is a project-finance tranche with a ticker. The skills transfer almost one-to-one — and that asymmetry is rare. This is the sleeve where you may be able to out-analyse the market with skills you already own, rather than skills you're still building.

How your project-finance toolkit maps to credit

  • DSCR & coverage modelling→Interest coverage, ability to service the coupon
  • Capital-structure seniority→Bond seniority, subordination, recovery in default
  • Covenant analysis→Bond indentures, protective covenants
  • IRR & discounting→Yield-to-maturity, yield-to-worst
  • Refinancing / tenor risk→Duration, maturity wall, rollover risk
  • Macro rate sensitivity→Yield curve, credit spreads, the credit cycle

What to actually build (Phase 4)

Learn the yield curve and what its shape implies, duration as your interest-rate exposure, and credit spreads as the market's pricing of default risk — note that your own macro watchlist already includes spreads > 700bps as a signal, which is exactly the credit-cycle lens Marks teaches.

Then do the concrete thing: open the IBKR bond desk, pick one investment-grade corporate, and underwrite it the way you'd underwrite a project tranche — coverage, seniority, spread vs. risk. Compare the market's yield to the compensation you'd demand. That single exercise will tell you whether this becomes a core part of your edge.

The Intelligent Investor

Benjamin Graham · 1949 · "the father of value investing"
▾
The author

Benjamin Graham was born Benjamin Grossbaum in London in 1894; his family emigrated to New York when he was a year old. His father died when Benjamin was nine, and the family slid from comfort into genuine poverty — his mother lost what remained in the Panic of 1907. That early fall from security into hardship marked him permanently: his life's work became, in effect, a system for never being financially destroyed again.

A brilliant student, he graduated near the top of his Columbia class and was offered teaching posts in three different departments; he chose Wall Street instead. He built the Graham-Newman partnership, was nearly wiped out in the 1929–32 crash (down roughly 70% before recovering), and emerged with his risk-first philosophy hardened into doctrine. He taught at Columbia for decades — Warren Buffett was his most famous student and the only one he ever gave an A+. Known as "the dean of Wall Street," he died in 1976. The Intelligent Investor is his book for the layperson; Buffett calls it the best book on investing ever written.

Principles & how to apply them
01Mr. Market
Imagine a business partner who appears every day and quotes a price to buy your shares or sell you his — and whose mood swings wildly between euphoria and despair. He is there to serve you, not to instruct you. On manic days he offers absurd prices for your holdings; on depressive days he'll sell you fine businesses for a fraction of their worth. The intelligent investor exploits his moods rather than catching them.
ApplyWhen a compounder you've conservatively valued at €100 trades at €60 in a panic, that is not new information about the business — it's Mr. Market having a bad day. Your job is to have done the valuation in advance so you can act on his fear instead of sharing it.
02Investment vs. speculation
Graham's definition: an investment operation is one that, on thorough analysis, promises safety of principal and an adequate return; everything else is speculation. The point isn't that speculation is immoral — it's that you must always know which one you are doing.
ApplyBuying an IPO on momentum because it might double is speculation, however exciting; buying a business below a defensible estimate of its worth is investment. Label every action honestly before taking it — trouble starts when you speculate while telling yourself you're investing.
03Margin of safety
The central idea of the entire book, which Graham distilled to three words. Always buy at a price meaningfully below your conservative estimate of value, so that errors in your analysis and ordinary bad luck don't become permanent loss. The discount is the protection against being wrong.
ApplyThis is identical in spirit to the coverage cushion you build into a project's DSCR — you don't underwrite to exactly 1.0x because reality disappoints. Demand the same buffer in equities: if your honest value is €100, your buy price is €65–70, not €98.
04Defensive vs. enterprising investor
Graham offers two honest paths. The defensive investor wants simplicity and freedom from effort, and should own a diversified, low-cost portfolio and largely leave it alone. The enterprising investor is willing to devote real time and temperament to outperform — and most people who think they're enterprising are not.
ApplyYour architecture already encodes both: the 50% ETF core is the defensive investor; the compounder sleeve is the enterprising one. The danger is being enterprising in name (lots of activity) without the work and patience the role actually demands.
05Be businesslike
"Investing is most intelligent when it is most businesslike." You own a fractional stake in a real enterprise, not a flickering ticker — approach it as you would buying the whole company: study the economics, the balance sheet, the durability.
ApplyBefore any purchase, write the one-sentence description of the business and ask: would I buy 100% of it at this valuation if I could never sell? If the ticker vanished and you only had the business, would you still want it?
06Protect, don't predict
Graham was deeply skeptical of forecasting. His defenses are structural — diversification, quality, and the margin of safety — not a superior crystal ball.
ApplyThe antidote to a macro instinct. Rather than positioning the portfolio for a forecasted recession, build one robust to many futures: a margin of safety on each holding, a permanent index core, and dry powder that exists because nothing is cheap — not because a crash is predicted.

The Most Important Thing

Howard Marks · 2011 · founder, Oaktree Capital
▾
The author

Howard Marks was born in 1946 and grew up in Queens, New York. He studied finance at Wharton, took an MBA at the University of Chicago, and spent his early career at Citibank — including a stretch running convertible and high-yield bonds, which pushed him toward the unloved corners of markets where mispricing lives.

In 1995 he co-founded Oaktree Capital Management, now one of the world's largest investors in distressed debt and credit (~$200bn). Since 1990 he has written periodic "memos" to clients — Warren Buffett has said that when he sees one in his inbox, he reads it first. His specialty is the psychology of cycles and risk; this book collects the ideas from those memos. The "illuminated" edition adds margin commentary from other investors, itself a lesson in second-level thinking.

Principles & how to apply them
01Second-level thinking
First-level thinking is simple and shared: "good company, buy the stock." Second-level thinking asks what everyone else already believes, what's therefore already in the price, and where your view differs and why you might be right. To beat the market you must be both different and correct — which feels uncomfortable, because consensus feels safe.
Apply"AI infrastructure will be huge" is first-level and almost certainly priced. The second-level questions: what growth rate is today's price implying, and is the market over- or under-estimating it? If you only know what everyone knows, you have no edge.
02No good or bad assets — only good or bad prices
A wonderful company can be a terrible investment if you overpay; a mediocre one can be a great investment if it's cheap enough. Value investing is simply the discipline of buying below intrinsic worth.
ApplyReplace "is this a good company?" with "is this a good price for this company?" Berkshire at 22x operating earnings and Berkshire at 1.4x book are completely different investments — same company.
03Risk = probability of permanent loss, not volatility
Academic finance equates risk with price wobble. Marks insists the real risk is losing money you don't get back. Counterintuitively, risk is highest when prices are high and everyone feels safe, and lowest when prices are low and everyone is frightened.
ApplyA beloved blue-chip at 40x earnings is riskier — more likely to permanently impair capital — than a hated, well-financed cyclical at 6x, even though the blue-chip's chart looks calmer. Judge risk by price-paid-vs-value, not by how smooth the line has been.
04The pendulum & market cycles
Markets rarely sit at "fair." They swing like a pendulum between greed and fear, too-high and too-low, spending most of their time travelling toward an extreme. You can't predict the timing, but you can know roughly where you are by reading the temperature.
ApplyThe resolution to your cash dilemma. Don't forecast the recession — assess whether prices, sentiment and credit embed optimism or fear, and become more aggressive as fear (and cheapness) rises. Cycle position, not the calendar, sets your aggressiveness.
05Contrarianism
To do better than the crowd you must act against it — buy when others sell in despair, sell when they buy in greed. The hard part is psychological, not analytical: it means standing apart while looking wrong for a while.
ApplyPrepare your buy list and target prices now, in calm weather, so that when a real dislocation comes and every instinct says "wait," you have pre-committed decisions to execute. Conviction built in advance is what lets you act when others freeze.
06We can't predict, but we can prepare
Marks divides investors into the "I know" school (confident forecasters) and the "I don't know" school (humble preparers) — and belongs firmly to the second. You don't need to know the future to invest well; you need a portfolio that survives many futures and a margin for error.
ApplyStop trying to call the macro top or bottom. Ask instead: "If I'm wrong about the cycle in either direction, does this portfolio still survive?" Defense and humility beat prediction.

Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits

Philip Fisher · 1958 · pioneer of growth-quality investing
▾
The author

Philip Fisher was born in San Francisco in 1907. He briefly attended Stanford's new business school, then began as a securities analyst in 1928 — just in time to witness the Crash. In 1931, in the depths of the Depression, he started his own firm, Fisher & Company, reasoning that clients burned by their existing brokers might be open to someone new.

He became the pioneer of the growth branch of value investing: rather than hunting statistically cheap "cigar butts," he sought a handful of genuinely superior businesses and held them for decades. His most famous purchase — Motorola in 1955 — he held until his death. Buffett, who sought Fisher out after reading him, describes himself as "85% Graham and 15% Fisher"; Fisher's qualitative lens is the half a quantitative investor most needs. He died in 2004 at 96.

Principles & how to apply them
01The fifteen points
Fisher offers fifteen qualitative questions to judge a business: products with years of runway, management committed to renewing the pipeline, productive R&D, a strong sales organisation, durable margins, good labour and executive relations, depth and — above all — unquestionable integrity of management.
ApplyTurn these into your standing moat checklist. For a smart-grid equipment maker, don't stop at the multiple — ask whether its R&D is genuinely productive, whether utilities are loyal customers, and whether management has ever been candid about a setback. Your 7-question gauntlet is the descendant of these fifteen.
02Scuttlebutt
Fisher's signature method: build conviction the way a journalist builds a story — by talking to customers, suppliers, competitors, and former employees. The business grapevine reveals truths no financial statement shows.
ApplyTo evaluate a defense or grid supplier, the most valuable hour isn't with the 10-K — it's spent on what its customers and competitors say and what ex-employees think. A rival grudgingly admitting "we can't beat them on X" is worth more than any ratio.
03When to buy
Fisher cared less about statistical cheapness and more about buying outstanding companies at reasonable prices before the market fully recognises their quality — often around moments of temporary, fixable difficulty.
ApplyWatch for excellent businesses hit by a transient problem — a recall, a guidance miss, a passing legal cloud — that frightens the crowd but doesn't impair the franchise. That's the growth investor's version of a margin of safety.
04When to sell — almost never
There are only three good reasons to sell: you made a mistake in the original analysis; the business has deteriorated and no longer meets the fifteen points; or you've found something dramatically better. Notice what's absent: "the price went up," "the market looks scary," "I'm bored."
ApplySelling a true compounder for any other reason is usually wealth-destroying — Fisher held Motorola through countless "expensive" moments across 40 years. When tempted to trim a winner because it's "had a run," check the three reasons; if none applies, do nothing.
05Don't over-diversify
Owning too many stocks guarantees you own businesses you don't understand well, diluting both your best ideas and your attention. Better to know a handful of superb companies deeply.
ApplyThis tensions productively with Bogle's indexing — resolve it as your architecture does: a broad index core for what you don't research, and genuine concentration (not 30 half-understood names) in the few businesses where you've done Fisher-grade work.
06Don't quibble over eighths and quarters
Having found a wonderful business at a sensible price, don't lose it by haggling over a trivial difference in entry price. The cost of missing a decades-long compounder dwarfs the few percent you tried to save.
ApplyIf your work says a great business is worth buying near €70 and it trades at €72, a patient limit order is fine — but refusing to ever own it because you anchored on €68 is the expensive mistake. Don't let a rounding error cost you a franchise.

Poor Charlie's Almanack

Charlie Munger · 2005 · Vice-Chairman, Berkshire Hathaway
▾
The author

Charlie Munger was born in Omaha, Nebraska, in 1924 — Buffett's hometown, in whose grandfather's grocery store he worked as a teenager, though the two wouldn't meet until 1959. He served as a meteorologist in the Army Air Corps, then talked his way into Harvard Law School without a bachelor's degree and graduated with distinction.

He practiced law, co-founded Munger, Tolles & Olson, then ran an investment partnership (1962–75) that compounded at nearly 20% a year. As Buffett's partner and Berkshire's Vice-Chairman, he is widely credited with moving Buffett from Graham's cigar-butts toward paying up for quality. A true polymath, he preached "worldly wisdom" — the disciplined use of big ideas from every field. He died in November 2023, weeks short of 100.

Principles & how to apply them
01The latticework of mental models
Hold a broad collection of fundamental models from many disciplines — psychology, physics, biology, maths, economics, history — and hang your experience on this latticework. "To the man with only a hammer, every problem looks like a nail." Single-discipline thinkers mis-solve most problems.
ApplyTo understand a business you need economics (moats), psychology (why customers stay), accounting (what the numbers hide) and maths (compounding, base rates). Analysing an AI-infrastructure firm, your energy/finance models cover part of it — deliberately recruit technology-adoption and psychology models for the rest.
02Inversion
"Invert, always invert." Many hard problems are best solved backward. Rather than "how do I get rich," ask "how do I avoid going broke," and avoid those things. Rather than "how does this succeed," ask "how does this fail."
ApplyThis is the pre-mortem — the most useful single habit in your process. Before buying, write: "It's five years from now and this has been a disaster. What happened?" The answers — a hidden debt wall, an overrated moat, a misjudged management — become the risks you monitor or the reasons you pass.
03The psychology of human misjudgment
Munger catalogues ~two dozen standardised causes of error: incentive-caused bias, liking/disliking distortion, doubt-avoidance, inconsistency-avoidance (we defend prior commitments), social proof, deprival super-reaction (losses hurt far more than equivalent gains), authority-misinfluence, and more. Knowing them lets you catch them — especially in yourself.
ApplyWhen you notice yourself only seeking confirming evidence, or holding a loser because selling means admitting error, name the bias out loud. Naming it breaks its grip. Keep his list beside your decision journal.
04"Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome"
Incentive-caused bias is, for Munger, the most powerful and underrated force in human affairs. People — advisors, managements, analysts — usually rationalise their way to whatever benefits them, often sincerely.
ApplyWhenever someone recommends an investment, product or corporate action, ask first: how are they paid, and what do they get if you say yes? A broker pushing a structured product, a CEO doing an empire-building acquisition — follow the incentive before weighing the argument.
05Circle of competence
Know the boundary of what you genuinely understand, and stay inside it. It's not the size of the circle that matters but knowing exactly where its edge is.
ApplyYour circle is energy, infrastructure and credit/capital-structure analysis. Biotech is almost certainly outside it. The skill isn't expanding the circle fast — it's the honesty to stamp "too hard, pass" on anything where you'd lose to a specialist, and the patience to wait for pitches inside your zone.
06Patience, then decisive concentration
Munger's "sit on your ass investing": the big money is not in the buying and selling but in the waiting. Great opportunities are rare; when one arrives within your competence at a fat-pitch price, you bet heavily, then do nothing for a long time.
ApplyThis legitimises patient cash — but only the Klarman kind (no bargains), not the macro-forecast kind. Wait, mostly idle, for the rare obvious mispricing in your circle; then size it meaningfully (within your caps) rather than spreading thin — and sit still while it compounds.
07Lollapalooza effects
When several models or biases all push the same way at once, you get extreme, non-linear outcomes — manias, panics, runaway successes.
ApplyRecognise when forces are stacking. A bubble is greed + social proof + envy + commitment + fear-of-missing-out firing together — exactly the cocktail around a hot IPO. Seeing the stack form is your cue to step back, because the crowd is about to behave at an extreme.

Value Investing: From Graham to Buffett and Beyond

Bruce Greenwald · 2001 · Columbia Business School
▾
The author

Bruce Greenwald is an economist and, for decades, the intellectual anchor of value investing at Columbia Business School, where he held the Heilbrunn Professorship of Asset Management and Finance and revived the Graham-and-Dodd tradition in the heart of Buffett's old academic home. Trained with a PhD from MIT, he was nicknamed "a guru to Wall Street's gurus" for the generation of professional value investors he taught.

His distinctive contribution is to make value investing rigorous and systematic — replacing fuzzy intuition with a disciplined three-layer valuation and a hard-edged theory of competitive advantage. Of the eight, this is the most analytically precise book, and it dovetails directly with your modelling background.

Principles & how to apply them
01Three sources of value, in order of reliability
Build a valuation from the most reliable component to the least: first asset value (reproduction cost of the assets), then earnings power value (current sustainable earnings, capitalised, assuming no growth), and only last the value of growth (least reliable, worth little without a moat).
ApplyReverse the usual order. Instead of starting with a growth-driven DCF — the shakiest layer, where small assumption changes swing the answer wildly — anchor first on reproduction cost and no-growth earnings power. You'll trust your number more, and you'll see exactly how much of the price depends on speculative growth.
02Earnings Power Value (EPV)
Take current, normalised, sustainable earnings (cleaned of cycles and one-offs, adjusted for maintenance capex) and capitalise them at the cost of capital — with no growth assumed. The result is a value that doesn't rely on forecasting the future.
ApplyFor a cyclical energy name, normalise earnings across a full cycle rather than off a peak or trough year, divide by your cost of capital, and you have a forecasting-free anchor value. This plays to your strength and removes the false precision of a long-dated growth DCF.
03The EPV-vs-asset-value diagnostic
Comparing the two reliable layers tells you what kind of business you hold. EPV ≈ asset value → no competitive advantage (returns just cover cost of capital). EPV >> asset value → a franchise, a real moat earning excess returns. EPV < asset value → management is destroying value.
ApplyRun this before trusting any moat story. If a company claims a wide moat but its EPV barely exceeds its asset value, the moat is in the deck, not the numbers. It's a quantitative lie-detector for "quality."
04Growth is only valuable behind a barrier to entry
In a competitive market with no moat, growth is worthless: new entrants compete returns down to the cost of capital, so growing merely consumes capital at no profit. Growth creates value only when a competitive advantage lets the company earn above its cost of capital on the new capital invested.
ApplyBefore paying any premium for "growth," ask: is there a barrier to entry protecting the returns on that growth? A fast-growing commodity producer with no moat is treading water on value; a moated franchise reinvesting at high ROIC is where the magic compounds. Don't pay growth multiples for unprotected growth.
05Competitive advantage = barriers to entry (and they're rare)
Real, durable advantages come down to a few sources: economies of scale combined with customer captivity, and occasionally proprietary technology or government protection. The empirical test is stability of market share and persistently high returns over many years.
ApplyTest any claimed moat against the evidence: have market shares been stable for years (incumbents can't be dislodged), and have returns on capital stayed high through a full cycle? Brand, "first-mover" and size alone usually aren't moats — demand the share-stability and durable-ROIC evidence.
06Hunt where institutions won't look
Mispricing concentrates where institutions can't or won't go — the boring, obscure, small, spun-off and distressed.
ApplyTilt idea-sourcing toward neglect: post-spinoff orphans, complex holding companies (a skill you already own — EXOR, the insurance vehicles), small-caps below institutional radar, forced-seller situations. Glamour is where overpaying lives; neglect is where bargains hide.

The Little Book of Common Sense Investing

John C. Bogle · 2007 · founder of Vanguard
▾
The author

John "Jack" Bogle was born in New Jersey in 1929, just before the Crash; his family lost its money in the Depression, and the frugality that defined him was forged early. At Princeton he wrote a senior thesis on the mutual-fund industry that effectively set the course of his life.

He rose to lead the Wellington fund, was fired after a bad merger, and turned that humiliation into the founding of The Vanguard Group in 1974 — structured, uniquely, to be owned by its own funds (and therefore its investors), removing the conflict between managers and clients. In 1976 he launched the first index mutual fund for ordinary people, mocked at the time as "Bogle's Folly." It became the most powerful wealth-building tool the individual investor has ever had. He died in 2019; Buffett says no one has done more for the individual investor.

Principles & how to apply them
01Don't look for the needle — buy the haystack
Rather than trying to pick the few winning stocks in advance, own the entire market through a single low-cost, broadly diversified index fund. You're guaranteed your fair share of the market's long-term return.
ApplyThe foundation of your 50% core. VWCE is the haystack — thousands of companies worldwide, auto-rebalancing, no decisions — ensuring you participate in global growth even when individual picks disappoint, which statistically many will.
02The relentless rules of humble arithmetic
Every euro paid in fees, turnover and taxes is a euro of return you don't keep — and it compounds against you exactly as returns compound for you. Over decades, a seemingly small annual cost quietly consumes an enormous share of your potential wealth.
ApplyTreat TER as a permanent headwind. The gap between a 0.20% global index fund and a 1.5% active fund, compounded over 30 years, can swallow a third or more of your final wealth — for, on average, worse performance. Minimise costs as ferociously as financing costs on a project.
03The Cost Matters Hypothesis
Sharper than efficient-markets theory: as a group, all investors earn the market return before costs; therefore, as a group, active investors must earn the market return minus their higher costs. It's arithmetic, not opinion.
ApplyThis is why most active funds lose to the index over 20 years — and it sets the bar for your own picking: your compounder sleeve must beat the cheap index after your effort and mistakes, or it isn't earning its place.
04Reversion to the mean
Today's hot fund or sector is tomorrow's laggard; outperformance tends to revert toward the average. Chasing last year's winners is a recipe for buying high.
ApplyWhen tempted to overweight whatever just soared (the AI names after a huge run, last year's best fund), remember performance reverts. Add new money proportionally to plan, not to the recent winner — the rule already written into your architecture.
05Emotion and market timing are the enemy
The index investor's edge is behavioural as much as mathematical: by staying the course through panics and manias, they avoid the buy-high-sell-low pattern that destroys most investors' returns.
ApplyThe core sleeve's most valuable function is removing the temptation to act. In a crash, the discipline is to keep contributing to VWCE, not sell it to "wait for clarity." The do-nothing default is the feature, not the limitation.
06The majesty of simplicity
One or two broad, low-cost index funds will, for most people most of the time, beat a complicated arrangement of clever products. Complexity mostly enriches the people selling it.
ApplyResist adding a fifth thematic ETF because a story is compelling. Keep the core boringly simple; spend your complexity budget on the compounder and credit work where you might actually have an edge.

Margin of Safety

Seth Klarman · 1991 · founder, The Baupost Group
▾
The author

Seth Klarman was born in 1957, studied economics at Cornell and took an MBA at Harvard, then founded The Baupost Group in Boston in 1982 at the age of 25. Over four decades he built one of the most successful and famously cautious value funds in the world, managing tens of billions while routinely holding large cash when he could find nothing worth buying.

His risk-aversion is legendary: he targets absolute returns and the avoidance of loss above all. He wrote Margin of Safety in 1991; it went out of print and became a cult object, with used copies selling for thousands — a fact that would amuse a man who built his career on the gap between price and value. Known as "the Oracle of Boston," Klarman is, of the eight, the author whose temperament most directly addresses your cash question.

Principles & how to apply them
01Risk-aversion first — absolute returns
Avoiding loss matters more than scoring gains, because losses do asymmetric damage: a 50% loss requires a 100% gain just to break even. Klarman measures himself against zero, not an index, which frees him from the pressure to be fully invested.
ApplySize every position so being wrong is survivable, never catastrophic. Ask not "how much could I make?" first, but "if this goes against me, what's the permanent damage, and can the portfolio absorb it?" Downside before upside, always.
02Cash is a residual, not a position
The principle that resolves your outstanding diagnostic. Klarman holds large cash not because he forecasts a crash, but because he is rigorously bottom-up and simply can't find enough things below conservative value. The cash is the absence of opportunity, not a market call.
ApplyTest yourself honestly. If your cash exists because you examined the businesses in your circle and none offers a margin of safety today, that's value investing — legitimate. If it exists because you have a recession probability and you're positioning for it, that's a macro bet in a value coat. Klarman is the first; make sure you are too.
03Bottom-up, not top-down
Klarman builds the portfolio security by security, focusing on the value of individual investments — not on macro forecasts about the economy or market, which he regards as a fool's errand that distracts from the analysable.
ApplyThe decision variable is never "will the S&P fall 20%." It's "is this specific business, which I understand, available below a conservative estimate of its worth, today?" Start every analysis from the security, not the scenario.
04Markets are inefficient because crowds are emotional
Value investing works precisely because prices are set by humans subject to fear, greed and institutional pressure — not rational machines. Mispricing is created by behaviour, and patience plus analysis is how you harvest it.
ApplyThe best opportunities come from forced or emotional selling — index deletions, post-bankruptcy distributions, spinoffs dumped by holders who never wanted them, panics. Look hardest exactly where others are selling for non-fundamental reasons.
05Wait for the pitch
Klarman will do nothing, sometimes for a long time, rather than lower his standards. The discipline to abstain is as important as the skill to analyse.
ApplyPairs with Munger's "sit on your ass investing." Your default state is patient inactivity within your circle, punctuated by decisive action when a genuine bargain appears. The hardest skill isn't analysis — it's the willingness to look "unproductive" while you wait.
06Process over outcome
Because markets are partly random short-term, a good decision can have a bad result and vice versa. Klarman judges himself on the quality and discipline of his process, not the outcome of any single bet.
ApplyIn your decision journal, grade each call on whether you followed your process — did the work, demanded the margin of safety, sized sensibly — independent of this quarter's P&L. A profit made by abandoning discipline is a warning sign, not a success.

Fooled by Randomness

Nassim Nicholas Taleb · 2001 · trader, essayist of uncertainty
▾
The author

Nassim Nicholas Taleb was born in 1960 in Amioun, Lebanon, into a once-prominent family whose fortunes were upended by the Lebanese civil war — an early, visceral education in how quickly apparent stability collapses, and how much history is shaped by rare, violent events. He earned an MBA at Wharton and a PhD in Paris, then spent years as a derivatives trader and quant specialising in options — instruments whose whole purpose is to profit from extreme, improbable moves.

He turned from trading to writing, producing the Incerto series (Fooled by Randomness, The Black Swan, Antifragile, Skin in the Game) — an extended argument about uncertainty, fat tails and human self-deception. Combative and iconoclastic, he is the necessary skeptic of the eight: the one who reminds you that much of what looks like skill is luck wearing a good story. Read this one just as you're about to size real money.

Principles & how to apply them
01Distinguish luck from skill
Much of what passes for skill, especially over short horizons, is randomness. A fund with a brilliant three-year run may simply be a lucky draw from a large population — indistinguishable, in the moment, from genuine talent.
ApplyThe inoculation against your own future success. After a few winning calls the seductive story is "I'm good at this," and the temptation is to size up. Resist it. Judge yourself by process and by a sample large enough to mean something — not by a handful of outcomes luck could fully explain.
02Survivorship bias & silent evidence
We see only the winners — the successful funds, the rich entrepreneurs, the bold bets that paid off. The far larger graveyard of those who took identical risks and were wiped out is invisible, so we overestimate the odds of the strategy that happened to work.
ApplyWhen you read about someone who got rich concentrating in one stock, remember the thousands who did exactly the same and lost everything — you never hear from them. Before copying a bold strategy, ask: how many who tried this are simply not around to write a book?
03Judge decisions by alternative histories
A decision's quality depends on the full distribution of what could have happened, not the single path that did. A reckless bet that paid off was still a bad decision; Russian roulette "works" five times out of six.
ApplyA profitable trade can be a terrible decision if it risked ruin to make a little. Reviewing your journal, evaluate each call against the range of outcomes it exposed you to — "given what I knew and risked, was this wise?" — not just "did it make money?"
04Rare events dominate — beware fat tails
Extreme, improbable events do most of the damage and most of the good; the world isn't the tidy bell curve risk models assume. Strategies that earn steady small profits while quietly risking catastrophic loss — "picking up pennies in front of a steamroller" — are traps.
ApplyBe deeply suspicious of any approach that makes money smoothly by, in effect, selling insurance against disasters (high leverage, shorting volatility, over-concentrated carry). The smooth equity curve is often the warning sign, not the reassurance. Survive the tail; don't optimise the calm middle.
05The narrative fallacy & overconfidence
Humans compulsively invent tidy stories to explain random events, and these stories breed false confidence. Market commentary explaining each day's move with a confident cause is mostly narrative imposed on noise.
ApplyDistrust explanations that are too neat — especially your own after a win. When you catch yourself constructing a confident story about why the market moved, or why your thesis is "obviously" right, treat that fluency as a yellow flag. The world is noisier than the story sounds.
06Avoid ruin above all
No matter how small the probability, never expose yourself to a risk that can wipe you out — over enough trials the unlikely becomes inevitable, and from ruin there is no recovery. Survival is the precondition of compounding.
ApplyThe hard floor under your whole portfolio. Never size a position, use leverage, or concentrate such that a plausible tail event ends your investing life. You can recover from being wrong many times; you cannot recover from being wiped out once. Ruin-avoidance always outranks return.
Beyond the core eight — the extended library
How these fit. The eight above are the canon you build judgment on. The books below deepen specific dimensions — deep-value rigor, the stock-picker's craft, the behavioral edge, and the boring-but-decisive mechanics of allocation and wealth. Same treatment throughout: who the author is, the principles, and how to apply each. Read them in the phase where they belong, not all at once.

Why Stocks Go Up and Down

William Pike & Patrick Gregory · 4th ed. · the mechanics primer
▾
The authors

William Pike spent a career teaching the actual machinery of securities to entry-level analysts and trainees, and built this book out of that material — it is the text many firms hand a new hire to make them literate before they touch a model. It is unusual in the canon: not a philosophy of investing but a ground-up manual of how securities and financial statements actually work, written so that nothing is taken on faith.

The fourth edition is co-authored with Patrick Gregory, CFA, a finance academic who updated and tightened the treatment of valuation and cash flow. The whole book is built around watching a single fictional company — JMC — get founded, borrow, issue stock, go public, and grow, so that you understand a balance sheet because you saw it assembled, not because you memorised its parts.

Principles & how to apply them
01Build the company before you read its statements
The book starts a business from one dollar and grows it page by page — equity in, debt on, assets bought — so the income statement and balance sheet appear as the natural record of real decisions, not abstract forms to be memorised.
ApplyYou already think this way in project finance — sources and uses, the waterfall, what each tranche funds. This is the same mental model applied to a listed operating company: read every statement as the residue of capital-allocation decisions.
02The denominator matters as much as the numerator
Going public, secondary offerings, convertibles and options all change the share count. Earnings per share — the thing the market actually prices — can fall even as earnings rise, purely through dilution.
ApplyThe exact lens you needed for the SpaceX IPO question: distinguish a primary raise (new shares, dilution, cash into the business) from a secondary (existing holders cashing out, no new capital). Always value per fully-diluted share, never headline earnings alone.
03Why bonds move — and what that teaches about every security
A clean, mechanical treatment of why a bond's price falls when rates rise, how duration and credit quality drive the move, and how convertibles and preferreds sit between debt and equity in the capital structure.
ApplyThis is the connective tissue to the credit edge already flagged as your rarest advantage. The chapters on bonds, converts and preferreds are the fastest way to map your DSCR-and-seniority instinct onto listed fixed income — read these even if you skim the rest.
04Cash flow is not earnings
Depreciation, capitalising versus expensing, and inventory accounting (FIFO vs LIFO) all open a gap between reported profit and the cash a business actually generates. The book walks each one through JMC's numbers until the gap is obvious.
ApplyThe single most important distinction for an owner-earnings investor — and the one accounting hides. When you compute owner-earnings for a compounder, this is the chapter that tells you which "profit" was real and which was an accounting choice.
05Valuation ratios are relationships, not magic numbers
P/E, EV/EBITDA and the rest are derived from first principles, so you see why enterprise value is the honest comparator across companies with different debt loads — the same logic Greenblatt uses for earnings yield.
ApplyTreat a multiple as a compressed DCF, not a verdict. A 12x P/E only means something once you know the growth, the reinvestment rate and the balance sheet behind it — which is precisely what your 7-phase framework forces you to unpack.
06In the end, price follows expected earnings and the discount rate
The closing chapter assembles everything: a stock rises or falls as the market revises its view of future earnings and the rate at which it discounts them. Everything earlier in the book is the vocabulary needed to read that revision.
ApplyUse this as a fast literacy gate, not a judgment book: skim what you already own from project finance, and read closely only the equity-specific plumbing — dilution, secondaries, converts, EV conventions — that your career gave you less reason to touch. It fills the gap, then gets out of the way.
Beginner primers — below your level, noted for completeness
Honest placement. These two are entry-level on-ramps for someone opening a brokerage account for the first time. Given your project-finance background, almost everything in them you already know — they're here for completeness and as a clean reference for terminology, not because they'll move your competence. Skim, don't study.

Security Analysis

Benjamin Graham & David Dodd · 1934 · the professional's bible
▾
The authors

This is the foundational text of the entire discipline. Graham — whom you met in the Intelligent Investor dossier — wrote it with David Dodd, his Columbia colleague and the meticulous academic who turned Graham's lectures into a systematic treatise. Published in 1934, in the wreckage of the Crash and Depression, it was the first serious attempt to put securities investing on a rigorous, analytical footing rather than treating it as speculation.

Where The Intelligent Investor is the accessible book for the layperson, Security Analysis is the dense professional manual — the one Buffett says he has read many times. The sixth edition carries a Buffett foreword. For someone with your modelling background, this is the rigorous deep end of the path.

Principles & how to apply them
01Value is determined independent of price
The analyst's job is to establish what a security is intrinsically worth from the fundamentals — assets, earnings power, dividends, financial strength — and only then compare it to the market price.
ApplyThis is the logic behind your 7-phase framework: do the valuation in isolation first, and let Mr. Market's quote be the last thing you look at, not the anchor you start from.
02Margin of safety, made quantitative
Graham & Dodd insist on a measurable cushion between price and value — especially in bonds and senior securities, where the focus is the issuer's ability to keep servicing obligations through bad years.
ApplyPure project-finance thinking: you don't underwrite to a coverage ratio that only holds in the best case. Demand the same measurable buffer in every security you buy.
03The net-net and asset-based floor
The famous "net current asset value" — buy below net working capital minus all liabilities, getting the fixed assets for free. Rare today, but the purest form of a margin of safety.
ApplyEven when true net-nets don't exist, the habit of grounding value in the balance sheet first (Greenwald's asset value) descends directly from here — a floor that doesn't depend on forecasts.
04Analyse the structure, not just the yield
For senior securities, study the covenants, the capital structure, and seniority — where you sit if things go wrong — not merely the headline coupon.
ApplyThe bond-credit lens you already own: read the indenture, locate yourself in the capital structure, and price the risk of the structure itself, exactly as you'd analyse a project tranche.
05Conservatism and the primacy of survival
Scarred by 1929, Graham & Dodd prize not losing over maximising. Protection of capital is the first duty of analysis.
ApplyThe through-line of the whole canon, stated at its origin: protect capital first; returns are what's left after you've made ruin impossible.

One Up on Wall Street

Peter Lynch · 1989 · Fidelity Magellan, ~29%/yr for 13 years
▾
The author

Peter Lynch ran Fidelity's Magellan Fund from 1977 to 1990 and produced one of the great track records in fund history — roughly 29% annualised over 13 years, growing the fund from about $18m to $14bn. Born in Boston in 1944, he lost his father young and started caddying to support the family — which is where he first overheard wealthy golfers talk stocks.

He's the great populariser of the idea that the ordinary investor holds real advantages over Wall Street. He retired early, at the top, to spend time with family and on philanthropy.

Principles & how to apply them
01Invest in what you know
Your everyday life as a consumer and professional lets you spot a great product or a thriving business before Wall Street's analysts do.
ApplyYour project-finance career is a scuttlebutt goldmine — you see which energy and grid technologies actually get deployed, and which suppliers utilities trust, long before it shows up in the financials.
02The six categories of stocks
Lynch sorts companies into slow growers, stalwarts, fast growers, cyclicals, turnarounds, and asset plays — and each demands a different thesis, valuation, and exit.
ApplyBefore sizing anything, classify it. A cyclical energy producer and a fast-growing grid-software compounder need completely different holding periods and valuation methods.
03The two-minute drill and the PEG ratio
You should be able to state why you own a stock in two minutes; and a fair P/E is roughly equal to the growth rate (the PEG idea).
ApplyIf you can't explain in two minutes why you own it and what would change your mind, you don't understand it yet — the same discipline as your one-sentence business description.
04Watch the story, not the price
Track whether the business thesis is actually playing out — earnings, store counts, margins — not the daily quote.
ApplyKeep a one-line thesis per holding and check the fundamentals each quarter, not the ticker each day. The quote is noise; the story is signal.
05The best stock to buy is often one you already own
Averaging into a winner whose story is still intact usually beats chasing a brand-new idea you understand less well.
ApplyWhen you have fresh capital, first ask whether your existing, well-understood holdings are still cheap — before reaching for something unfamiliar.

The Little Book That Still Beats the Market

Joel Greenblatt · 2010 · Gotham Capital · the "Magic Formula"
▾
The author

Joel Greenblatt founded Gotham Capital in 1985 and compiled an extraordinary record — reportedly around 40% annualised over its first two decades. He's also an adjunct professor at Columbia Business School, squarely in the Graham–Dodd lineage.

He wrote this book, famously, to be simple enough for his own children to understand — its whole argument is built around a kid named Jason selling gum. Behind the folksy story sits serious quantitative value investing.

Principles & how to apply them
01The Magic Formula — good and cheap, ranked
Systematically buy good businesses at bargain prices by ranking companies on two factors — return on capital (quality) and earnings yield (cheapness) — and buying the best combined ranks across 20–30 names.
ApplyThis is your 7-phase framework compressed to two numbers — ROIC for quality, EBIT/EV for price. Use it as a screen to surface candidates inside your circle, then do the deep work on the survivors.
02Earnings yield as EBIT/EV, not P/E
Greenblatt deliberately uses operating earnings over enterprise value because it neutralises differences in debt and tax, letting you compare a leveraged and an unleveraged company on equal footing.
ApplyThe cleanest cheapness metric for someone who thinks in capital structures — it prices the whole business, debt included, exactly as you'd underwrite a deal.
03High return on capital is the engine of compounding
A business earning high returns on tangible capital can reinvest its profits at those same high rates — the gum-shop earning 50% on each new store.
ApplyThis is the engine behind every compounder in your library: a company that redeploys earnings at 50% is worth vastly more than one stuck reinvesting at 6%.
04It works because it often doesn't
The formula underperforms for stretches of years — which is precisely why it keeps working. Most people can't stomach the wait and abandon it, so the edge survives.
ApplyThe behavioral moat. Any strategy easy to follow gets arbitraged away; the discipline to hold through multi-year underperformance is the source of the return.
05Conviction from understanding, not from the record
Greenblatt insists you must grasp why it works, or you'll quit at the first bad stretch — the good track record alone won't hold you.
ApplyNever follow a strategy — or a thesis — you can't defend from first principles. Borrowed conviction breaks under pressure exactly when you need it.

You Can Be a Stock Market Genius

Joel Greenblatt · 1997 · Gotham Capital · special situations
▾
The author

The same Joel Greenblatt behind the Magic Formula book above — but this is the earlier, harder, and far more interesting work. At Gotham Capital he compiled a record reported at roughly 40% annualised over two decades, and he did it not with a screen but by hunting in the market's neglected corners: spinoffs, restructurings, merger securities, recapitalisations. The clumsy title hides a serious book; it is the one many professional special-situations investors name as their formative text.

His argument is that the individual investor's real advantage is not competing with Wall Street on big, liquid, heavily-followed names — it is fishing where the institutions can't or won't: positions too small, too obscure, or too operationally messy for a large fund to bother with. Do your own work in those waters, he says, and you don't need to be smart so much as willing to look where no one else is looking.

Principles & how to apply them
01The profits hide in special situations
Greenblatt's secret hiding places are corporate events — spinoffs, mergers, bankruptcies, recapitalisations, rights offerings. They are mispriced not because they're complex but because the people who hold them are often forced or indifferent sellers, dumping for reasons that have nothing to do with value.
ApplyThis is a genuine extension of your circle, not a detour from it. A restructuring or a stub stock is a capital-structure puzzle — exactly what your project-finance career trained you to read. The edge your profile already flags in credit lives in these same waters.
02Spinoffs are the richest ground
When a parent spins off a division, index funds and institutions sell the small new orphan mechanically — they never wanted it. Meanwhile the spun-off managers, finally free and freshly incentivised with equity, often unlock value the conglomerate suppressed.
ApplyWatch the energy and industrial complex you already understand: forced break-ups, grid and infrastructure carve-outs, and "bad-bank" separations throw off exactly these orphans. Read the insider-ownership terms in the spin documents — that's where the signal is.
03Look down before you look up
Greenblatt's first question is never the upside — it is "how much can I lose, and how likely is that?" He sizes the downside, demands a margin of safety, and only then considers the reward. The exotic security is never a licence to speculate.
ApplyYour own discipline, in his words. The 7-question gauntlet asks for downside before upside; this book is a whole methodology built on that order. A spinoff you don't understand is speculation no matter how clever the structure looks.
04Concentrate — diversification past a point hides ignorance
Because each special situation carries its own structural margin of safety, Greenblatt argues for concentration: a handful of well-understood positions, not thirty half-understood ones. Holding eight names you've done real work on is safer than a basket you barely know.
ApplyThe counterweight to your wide interest list. This is the alpha sleeve's logic: a few high-conviction situations you've underwritten cold — sized deliberately — sitting on top of the permanent, broadly-diversified ETF core that needs no such work.
05Engineer the payoff with the right instrument
Merger securities, stub stocks, LEAPS, warrants and options let you shape an asymmetric bet — capping downside while keeping upside — but only once you've valued the underlying business. The instrument is a tool for expressing a view, never a substitute for having one.
ApplyTreat these the way you'd treat tranching a deal: the structure allocates risk and return, it doesn't create value. Decide what the asset is worth first; reach for the LEAP or the stub only to express that conclusion more efficiently.
06Your smallness is the edge
A multi-billion-dollar fund can't move the needle on a tiny spinoff and won't staff the research; that structural indifference is precisely the individual's advantage. The opportunity exists because the big money can't be bothered.
ApplyA rare case where being a solo EUR investor on IBKR is an asset, not a handicap. You can take a meaningful position in a situation too small for any institution to touch — provided you've done the work they skipped.

The Ten Commandments of Investment Management — the pocket catechism

The tight, fund-manager cut of the philosophy below, from his Boston College talk (delivered in Old-Testament deadpan: Moses dropped the real tablets, so God gave Pabrai the do-over, this time aimed at investment managers). These ten are the pocket catechism; the thirty models that follow are the full theology. Note the braided cluster — IV, V and II: hunt bargains so obvious (a P/E of 1) that the math is trivial in your head (no Excel), which means you need no analyst army (no team). Obviousness is the thread.

✦Thou shall… (the ten, in his order)
  • IThou shall not skim off the top. No fixed management fee — a performance fee only above a hurdle, the Buffett-partnership model. Win only when your investors win.
  • IIThou shall not have an investment team. Investing is a single-player game; an analyst army builds the very models you shouldn't need.
  • IIIThou shall accept you'll be wrong at least a third of the time. Even the great miss one in three — the framework must tolerate a high error rate.
  • IVThou shall look for hidden P/E-of-1 stocks. The whole company for one year's earnings — asset- or cash-rich situations that can become ten-baggers.
  • VThou shalt never use Excel. If you can't do the math on the fingers of one hand, the answer is no — and reaching for the spreadsheet is itself the sell signal.
  • VIThou shall always have a rope to climb out of the deepest well. Build the resilience to hold your nerve in a drawdown — his 2008 intrinsic-value spreadsheet was the rope, the one time he broke commandment V.
  • VIIThou shall be singularly focused like Arjuna. See only the economics of the business; macro and political noise are distractions from what matters.
  • VIIIThou shall never short a stock. Value investing hunts asymmetric upside; shorting caps the gain and uncaps the loss.
  • IXThou shall not introduce leverage. Leverage is what wiped out great investors when the storm finally came.
  • XThou shall be a shameless cloner. Don't invent ideas — clone the best from those who have already done the work.
IThe foundation — go all-in on simple ideas
  • 01The bedrock: take a simple idea and take it seriously. Nothing else works without this — the model most people understand and still fail to follow, because it demands going to an uncomfortable extreme.
IIThe Graham core — but do not overdose
  • 02Graham's fundamentals: a stock is a piece of a business; Mr. Market is there to serve you, not advise you; demand a margin of safety.
  • 03Do not overdose on Graham. His confession: "too much Ben Graham, too little Charlie Munger" was his career's biggest mistake — cigar-butt discipline makes you cut your flowers. Overlay Poor Charlie's Almanack, Fisher, and Pulak Prasad.
IIIConcentration & the brutal math of markets
  • 04The 20-punch card: imagine only 20 investments in a lifetime — one bet every 3–5 years. Fast is slow. Make few bets, thoughtfully.
  • 06A high error rate is guaranteed. You will be wrong more than half the time — the framework must tolerate it.
  • 07The 4% rule: over 90 years, ~4% of companies delivered all the market's net return. Even Buffett: ~12 decisions out of 300–400 built Berkshire. Own the few great ones.
  • 08Don't cut flowers and water weeds. A great manager's top 2–3 positions should be 70–80% of the book. Let winners reach escape velocity and run (Nick Sleep let Amazon become 80% and still won).
IVCircle of competence & the "too hard" pile
  • 05Stay in the epicentre. The size of the circle doesn't matter; staying in its centre does. (Arrillaga: real estate within one mile of Stanford, his whole life — a billionaire.) And 99%+ of ideas belong in Buffett's "too hard" box. If intrinsic value isn't obvious, you're outside your circle.
VIdea sourcing — clone shamelessly, hunt needles
  • 09Be a shameless cloner. Don't invent ideas — "the brain is a dangerous place." Mine Value Investors Club, Dataroma, SumZero; let an idea pass through another brain's filter first.
  • 10History rhymes. The 1960s–70s opportunity set is gone in the US — but lives elsewhere (Turkey today resembles the US mid-70s). Hunt where the mispricing still exists.
  • 17Enjoy hunting needles in haystacks. The 12-year-old Buffett sifting discarded racetrack tickets is the same man turning every page of Moody's manuals and the Japan Company Handbook. Read the footnotes; turn every page.
VIThe asymmetry hunt — your seam
  • 23Be a Swiss army knife — keep several specialised tools ready.
  • 24–26Spin-offs (Greenblatt), uber-cannibals (relentless buyback machines — AutoZone, NVR), and spawners (Amazon, Alphabet, Constellation) — three fertile hunting grounds.
  • 27Arbitrage is wonderful. Merger arb when you can read the outcome others can't (Buffett on Murdoch / Dow Jones).
  • 28Heads I win, tails I don't lose much. IPSCO: $40 price, $15/share cash plus two more years of $15 coming, no debt, plant free — downside muted, upside open. It ran to $160.
  • 29Low risk, high uncertainty — his signature. Wall Street overpays for predictability (ADP) and flees uncertainty it can't price. Fish where risk is low but uncertainty is high; the market misprices it. This is the exact seam your cash-and-wait, cyclical-entry strategy fishes.
VIIBehavioural discipline — not being your own enemy
  • 11Explain it to a 10-year-old in four sentences, or don't invest.
  • 12Always keep a rope for the deepest well. In 2008–09, down ~67%, he re-valued his book at intrinsic (not market) price in Excel and looked at it each morning to stay sane — a temporary, deliberate reality-distortion field to survive the panic.
  • 14Thou shalt not use Excel (except at the bottom of the well). If you can't do the math in your head, pass.
  • 15Use a pre-investment checklist — his has grown to ~213 questions, built from the real mistakes of great investors. The three biggest killers, in order: leverage, a misjudged moat, questionable management.
  • 16Be singularly focused like Arjuna — see only the eye of the bird.
  • 19Always have someone to discuss ideas with (Munger's rule; his was Li Lu, Guy Spier).
  • 20The mistress looks hotter than the wife. What you don't own looks better than what you do — but the wife (your holdings) is actually better. Don't chase shiny objects.
  • 21Neither a short-term borrower nor a long-term lender be — but short-term lender and long-term borrower is fine.
  • 22Introduce randomness into your life — the Heathrow book that changed everything came from randomness, not a plan.
VIIIHold the winners — quality, destiny, fees
  • 13Pursue quality intensely (Nick Sleep; Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance) — the highest-quality businesses, in management and moat, carry you to the promised land.
  • 18Your deepest desire is your destiny. Go all-in on what genuinely excites you; that becomes the opportunity — not chasing what you predict will be hot.
  • 30Do not skim off the top. If your structure allows, win only when your investors win (the 0 / 6 / 25 partnership model).

Masterclass · the latticework

Charlie Munger — the latticework of mental models

The source grid the Pabrai masterclass descends from. Munger's claim was never "learn some models" — it was that worldly wisdom is a latticework: take the big, durable ideas from every major discipline and hang them on one mental trellis, so a problem can be run past twenty lenses at once instead of one.

The man with one model is the proverbial fellow with a hammer, to whom every problem looks like a nail. The power is never a single model — it is the Lollapalooza effect: when several models point the same way at once, the forces compound into an outcome far larger than the sum of its parts. (That effect is the engine of the Pabrai masterclass above; this is where it was forged.)
Provenance — where the idea was forged, not just filed

The latticework was developed in a speech, not a book. Munger laid it out in his 1994 USC Business School talk, "A Lesson on Elementary, Worldly Wisdom" — the true primary source, where he said you must hang your experience on a latticework of models or end up the man with a hammer. He refined it in later talks (Worldly Wisdom Revisited, 1996; Harvard Law, 1998); the companion keystone — The Psychology of Human Misjudgment and its ~25 tendencies — was a separate speech of the same vintage.

Poor Charlie's Almanack (compiled by Peter Kaufman, 2005) is the anthology, not the birthplace — published roughly a decade later, it collects those speeches, wraps them in biography, and gives the framework its durable home. It is the best single compiled source, and the one to own — but the forge is the 1994 transcript, not the bound edition.

The wider lineage: Robert Hagstrom's Latticework: The New Investing (2000, later retitled Investing: The Last Liberal Art) built a whole book on the metaphor, and Shane Parrish's Great Mental Models series turned it into a mass-market curriculum — but the seed is Munger's, spoken aloud at USC in 1994.

Psychology
the model he weighted heaviest — ~25 ways the mind fools itself (The Psychology of Human Misjudgment)
  • Incentive-caused bias — "show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome." His master key: never ask what someone says, ask what they're paid to do.
  • Social proof — we copy the herd, worst of all under stress. The engine of bubbles.
  • Authority bias — we defer to the man in the white coat even when he's wrong.
  • Commitment & consistency — once we've said it aloud we defend it past the evidence (why you write the thesis down, then attack it).
  • Availability — we overweight the vivid and the recent over the statistically true.
  • Deprivation super-reaction (loss aversion) — a loss hurts far more than an equal gain; we go briefly insane near the point of losing.
  • Envy — "it is not greed that drives the world, but envy." The quiet destroyer of judgment.
  • Liking / disliking distortion — we excuse the faults of what we love and the virtues of what we hate.
Mathematics
not calculus — the practical arithmetic of reality
  • Compound interest — understanding it is the first rule of a happy life, financial and otherwise.
  • Probability & decision trees — think in expected values, not certainties.
  • Permutations & combinations — the Pascal/Fermat arithmetic of how likely things really are.
  • Law of large numbers — knowing when a sample is too small to mean anything.
Inversion
from algebra, but a master tool in its own right
  • Invert, always invert — solve the problem backwards. Instead of asking how to succeed, ask how you'd guarantee failure, then avoid exactly that. The literal engine of your pre-mortem.
  • Avoid, don't seek — to help a nation he'd ask how to ruin it, then do the opposite. Ruin-avoidance beats brilliance.
Accounting
a model — and the discipline to know its limits
  • Double-entry — accounting as a language of business, and a tool rather than the territory.
  • Its limits — depreciation is a real cost; "EBITDA" is a word he treats as a lie detector. The backbone of the forensic-accounting shelf in this library.
Microeconomics
where the money is actually made — the business lens
  • Competitive advantage / the moat — the durable structural edge that lets a business keep its excess returns.
  • Economies of scale — the advantages (and the traps) of size.
  • The surfing model — catch a big structural wave early and you look like a genius for a long time.
Biology & ecology
markets as living systems
  • Darwinian selection — markets as ruthless selection; most moats eventually die, because capitalism is everyone trying to take your business from you. (Pulak Prasad's Darwin, already on your shelf, is this beam of the trellis.)
Physics & engineering
how systems hold — and how they break
  • Critical mass / tipping points — the threshold past which a reaction becomes self-sustaining.
  • Redundancy & margin of safety — the engineer's backup system, which Graham borrowed for investing.
  • Breakpoints — systems hold until a threshold, then fail all at once.
Statistics
the shape of the real world, held with humility
  • Regression to the mean — extraordinary results drift back to average; don't extrapolate the exceptional.
  • Bell-curve humility — Gaussian thinking, held knowing the tails are fatter than the curve admits (where Munger and Taleb shake hands).
Your lens — the list is the easy half

Munger's real instruction was never "memorize thirty models." It was two behavioural commandments most people quietly skip. First: build the latticework across disciplines, not within one. The edge lives in the intersections — where incentives, accounting and competition all bear on the same decision. A finance-only mind is the man with the hammer; your nuclear-engineering background is an asset here precisely because it's a different beam on the trellis.

Second: weaponise inversion and incentives first. If you only ever ran two Munger models on every investment — "what are the incentives?" and "how would this fail?" — you would capture most of the value. The other twenty-eight refine; these two decide.

The bloodline. This is the source grid Pabrai's thirty models descend from, and it threads through your whole library: the biology beam is Prasad's Darwin, the accounting beam is the forensic shelf, the margin-of-safety beam is Graham. The best compiled sources already sit on your shelves — Poor Charlie's Almanack (the 2005 anthology; the Psychology of Human Misjudgment speech is its keystone chapter) and Griffin's Charlie Munger: The Complete Investor, which systematises the method.

Masterclass · the inflation doctrine

Warren Buffett — the inflation playbook (& the 1977 source text)

The capstone of the inflation material, and the essay beneath the clips: Buffett's 1977 Fortune piece, How Inflation Swindles the Equity Investor, plus his 2011 shareholder answer on which assets actually survive inflation. It quietly dismantles the comfortable belief that stocks are an inflation hedge — and it lands squarely on the one regime, and the one sector, you are positioned around.

The heretical claim: a stock is a disguised bond. Everyone knows a bond's fixed dollar coupon gets devoured by inflation. Buffett's move is to show equities carry a fixed coupon too — it's just hidden. The evidence is the one number that refuses to move: aggregate corporate return on equity sits near 12% across decades, high inflation and low alike. If the business world reliably earns ~12% on book and can't push higher, owning the market is economically a perpetual 12% bond — and inflation confiscates the equity coupon exactly as it does a bond's.
The asset ranking — what actually survives inflation (2011)
Tier 1 · the winnerCapital-light + pricing power

The toll booth that reprices with the currency but sinks no new capital to do it. See's can double its price and barely add a receivable or a brick. The ideal inflation assets: a pricing-power consumer franchise (See's, Coke), a royalty stream (a check on someone else's sales — no receivables, no inventory, no fixed assets), and — Buffett is explicit — your own earning power, which reprices upward with inflation at zero additional capital.

Tier 2 · mixedThe irreplaceable hard asset

The railroad, the world-class utility. Their replacement value soars with inflation (nobody rebuilds 22,000 miles of track cheaply) and the country always needs them — "not all bad to be world-class in your main businesses." But they are capital-hungry and earn a bond-like, near-fixed return: to grow a utility you sink ever-dearer capital per kilowatt and collect a regulated coupon.

Tier 3 · the swindledCapital-heavy & fixed-claim

Businesses drowning in receivables and inventory — flat unit volume, but now you must fund double the dollars just to stand still — and plain bonds, the pure fixed nominal claim inflation exists to devour.

The See's proof, in his own arithmetic
Bought at roughly $30M of volume on $9M of tangible assets; today well over $300M on ~$40M. He plowed back about $30M and it threw off on the order of $1.5 billion pre-tax. Across the span the dollar lost ~80–85% of its value, yet See's grew revenue roughly 10× while selling only ~75% more pounds — the rest was pure pricing power, captured on almost no incremental capital. That gap is the inflation edge.
The 1977 essay — why the 12% coupon won't rise (the five locked doors)

Buffett pre-empts the optimist's hope that ROE will climb to match inflation. There are only five ways to lift return on equity — and he walks through each, slamming it shut:

  • 1Higher asset turnover. Modest at best — LIFO and slow-repricing fixed assets barely moved the FORTUNE 500 from 1.18 to 1.29.
  • 2Cheaper leverage. No — inflation makes lenders more demanding and debt dearer, not cheaper.
  • 3More leverage. The gun is nearly empty (equity fell from 63% to 50% of assets), and new debt costs far more than the old it replaces.
  • 4Lower taxes. Government holds the "Class A, B and C stock" on every company's earnings and will not vote to shrink its own share.
  • 5Wider margins. The evidence says margins fell during inflation — even oil and steel couldn't price to replacement cost.

The knife: the disappointment comes not because the market falls, but in spite of the fact that it rises. The Dow can double over a decade and leave a seller, after capital-gains tax and inflation, worse off than today — more dollars, no more purchasing power. The damage is invisible: 6% inflation is the economic equivalent of a punitive income tax that nobody notices.

Folded in — Book value vs. earning power

Book value tells you what a business cost; intrinsic value tells you what it will earn. Book value is the accountant's record of capital poured in; intrinsic value is the discounted stream of owner-earnings that comes out. Buffett considers only the second — and the better the business, the wider the gap between them.

The counterintuitive hinge: the best businesses earn very high returns on little capital, so their true value vastly exceeds book (See's: worth over a billion on $40M of assets). A stock trading below book is usually not a bargain — it is the market signalling that those assets will earn poor returns. Book value "matters" mainly for lousy businesses, as a liquidation floor, and as a fraud-or-distress check.

Book value — a credit concept
When you are the lender (DSCR, asset coverage, the bond scorecard), the balance sheet is your floor and your friend — it is what you seize if it all goes wrong.
Earning power — an equity concept
When you are the owner (the ETF, the compounder), the balance sheet is history and the income statement is the future. Value the earning power of the steel, not the steel.

This is the same graft the Pabrai masterclass keeps hammering — underdose Graham, overdose Munger: book-as-margin-of-safety was Graham; earning-power-and-moat-as-margin-of-safety is Munger. And it is a live trap for you: project finance trains you to revere the balance sheet because lenders really are secured by steel in the ground — a strength in credit analysis, a liability in equity analysis. Hold both lenses; never confuse which seat you are in.

Your lens — the essay runs straight into your thesis

The sector tension. Read the ranking against your own watch-list — energy, utilities, smart grid, infrastructure, nuclear — and most of it sits in Tier 2 at best, with new-build in the swindled zone. Buffett spends paragraphs on exactly this: the utility paying a dividend with one hand while begging shareholders to mail the money back with the other, because inflation leaves no real retained earnings after funding the inflation top-up on its asset base. Your circle of competence overlaps inflation's cross-hairs. Hold two rescuing distinctions: owning an irreplaceable built asset (the railroad, a finished dam) is the Tier-2 winner, while building one (EPR2, your day job) pours ever-dearer capital for a capped return — and your own nuclear expertise is already a Tier-1 inflation asset.

The prophecy. Buffett's closing line, written in 1977, names "under-investment, stagflation, and the failures of the private sector" — your exact 2026 thesis, forty-nine years early, as the end-state of this mechanism. And he predicts governments will then direct capital into industry. Energy and industrial policy steering money into nuclear and grid is that predicted reaction — so your sector thesis is a bet on the policy response to the very disease this essay diagnoses.

The cross-link. This pairs with the inflationary-regime card in the Debt-crisis tab: that card tells you who gets hurt (bondholders, fixed-return utilities); this essay tells you what to own instead — pricing power on light capital. And it sharpens your bond discipline: if even equities are disguised bonds yielding a real zero under 7% inflation, a nominal fixed-coupon bond in that regime is a guaranteed loss of purchasing power.

▾
The author

Burton Malkiel is a Princeton economist and the most influential popular advocate of the efficient-market hypothesis and index investing. First published in 1973 and updated through many editions, the book argues that prices largely follow a "random walk" and that most investors are best served by low-cost index funds.

Principles & how to apply them

ApplyTake this as the bar you must clear: your active picks must beat a cheap index after costs and your mistakes, or indexing is the rational default.
02The random walk

ApplyIgnore charts and momentum as a basis for decisions. Your edge, if any, lives in valuation and patience — not in predicting next week.
03Index funds for most investors

ApplyThe academic backbone of your 50% ETF core — and a humbling check on whether the compounder sleeve is truly earning its keep.
04Bubbles still happen

ApplyA productive tension: markets are hard to beat and occasionally wildly mispriced. Act only in the rare windows where the mispricing is clear and inside your circle.

Warren Buffett's Ground Rules

Jeremy Miller · 2016 · the partnership letters, 1956–1970
▾
The author

Jeremy Miller is a financial professional and writer who undertook a careful study of Warren Buffett's partnership letters from 1956 to 1970 — the period before Berkshire, when Buffett ran an investment partnership and compounded partners' money at an extraordinary rate with no down years. Miller organises the previously hard-to-find letters into the "ground rules" Buffett set with his partners.

This is the purest record of Buffett's thinking before he became a public figure — the young Graham disciple putting the principles to work. For you, it's the bridge between Graham's theory and Buffett's practice.

Principles & how to apply them
01Measure against a benchmark, honestly
Buffett's first ground rule: judge performance against the market (then the Dow), over multi-year periods — not in absolute terms and not against zero. He promised to beat it by a margin over time, not every single year.
ApplyDefine your benchmark honestly (a global index for the equity sleeve) and judge yourself over 3–5 years against it. Don't mistake a rising market for skill, or a flat year for failure.
02The compounding obsession
The letters are full of compounding tables; the entire point is a high rate sustained over a long time, never interrupted.
ApplyInternalise that a few points of annual outperformance, sustained, is life-changing — and that interrupting compounding by sitting out is the cardinal sin.
03Generals, workouts, and controls
Buffett split the portfolio into undervalued "generals" (passive value bets), "workouts" (special situations — mergers, liquidations, spinoffs with a defined catalyst and timeline, largely uncorrelated to the market), and "controls" (taking a stake large enough to force value).
ApplyThe "workouts" idea speaks directly to your cash question: catalyst-driven, market-uncorrelated situations are how Buffett stayed productive when the market was expensive — a more sophisticated answer than simply holding cash and waiting.
04Conservatism is tested in down markets
Buffett told partners his real test was relative performance in declining markets; protecting capital in the bad years is what compounds.
ApplyJudge your own approach by how it holds up when markets fall, not by how it soars in a boom. Defense is where compounding is won or lost.
05Be a businessman investor; ignore forecasts
Buffett refused to predict the market and focused relentlessly on individual undervaluation.
ApplyThe Graham–Klarman bottom-up discipline, from the man who proved it at scale: find specific mispriced businesses; don't position the portfolio for a macro forecast.

The Myth of the Rational Market

Justin Fox · 2009 · a history of an idea and its hubris
▾
The author

Justin Fox is a financial journalist — a long-time Time columnist, later editorial director at Harvard Business Review and a Bloomberg columnist. This is not a how-to book; it is an intellectual history of the Efficient Market Hypothesis, tracing the idea from Bachelier and Samuelson through Fama's Chicago school, the CAPM and Black–Scholes, to its cracks under Shiller and the behaviorists and its humbling in 2008.

For a quantitatively-minded investor, it is the best single account of where the theory that says you can't win came from, how far it overreached, and exactly where it breaks — which is the seam value investing lives in.

Principles & how to apply them
01The efficient-market idea, in its strongest form
Prices reflect all available information, so the market price is the best estimate of value and beating it is near-impossible. Fox lays out the argument at full strength before showing its limits — the intellectually honest order.
ApplyThis is the case against your stock-picking sleeve, and you should be able to make it better than its defenders. It is also the justification for your permanent ETF core: concede efficiency where you have no edge.
02‘Rational’ quietly turned from a finding into an assumption
The theory's elegance led a generation to treat market rationality as axiomatic rather than tested — and to build risk models on top of that assumption.
ApplyMarkets are mostly efficient, occasionally not. The value investor earns nothing in the ‘mostly’ and everything in the ‘occasionally’ — so respect how often the market is right before betting it's wrong.
03The volatility puzzle (Shiller's crack)
Robert Shiller showed prices swing far more than the present value of future dividends can justify — markets are too volatile to be perfectly rational. This is the empirical wedge the whole behavioral school drove through.
ApplyThis is the academic licence for valuation itself, and for Faber's CAPE: if price oscillates around value far more than fundamentals do, then buying cheap and selling dear has a real, measurable edge.
04Models become reality — and then bite
Portfolio insurance, Value-at-Risk and Gaussian-copula models didn't just describe markets, they reshaped behavior and manufactured the fragility that broke in 1987 and 2008.
ApplyYour project-finance instinct already distrusts a model that assumes the stability it's meant to measure. Never let an elegant risk model lull you into the very exposure it can't see.
05The synthesis: neither perfectly efficient nor perfectly mad
Fox lands on humility on both sides — markets are smarter than the cranks think and dumber than the theorists hoped.
ApplyIndex the part you cannot beat; do real work only where you have a defensible edge. The honest middle is your entire operating thesis.

Global Value

Meb Faber · 2014 · Cambria Investment Management
▾
The author

Meb Faber is co-founder and CIO of Cambria Investment Management, author of The Ivy Portfolio and Shareholder Yield, a CAIA and CMT with an engineering background. He is one of the clearest quant-value writers working, and this short book is the most directly useful in this batch for your situation.

Its argument: use the cyclically-adjusted P/E (CAPE) — Graham & Dodd's smoothed-earnings idea, popularised by Shiller — not on one market but across 44 countries, to tilt toward cheap markets and away from expensive ones. It is the bridge between your macro anxiety and a disciplined, bottom-up-compatible process.

Principles & how to apply them
01CAPE — a market-level margin of safety
Smooth ten years of real earnings to strip out the cycle, then compare price to that. A high CAPE means you're paying a lot for each unit of normalised earnings; a low one means you're paying little.
ApplyTreat CAPE as the index-level version of the margin of safety you already demand on single names. As a EUR investor, the European and emerging CAPEs — not the heavily-watched US number — are your actual hunting map.
02Valuation predicts long-run returns, not next quarter
Faber's central chart is an almost perfect stair-step: high starting CAPE → low real 10-year returns, low CAPE → high returns. But it says nothing about the next few months. Markets can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent.
ApplyThis is the resolution to your cash tension. CAPE licenses calibrating aggressiveness to valuation over years — not selling everything because a recession might come. Strategic guide, never a short-term timing trigger.
03‘You are a bad investor’ — the behavioral tax
DALBAR data show the average investor badly underperforms the funds they own, because they buy at euphoric tops and sell at terrified bottoms. Sentiment peaks exactly when valuation peaks.
ApplyThe case for your written Portfolio Policy Statement. A rules-based process exists precisely to overrule the version of you that will want to sell in the panic you're currently waiting for.
04Go global; the cheapest countries win; buy a basket
Home bias is universal and costly. Sorting countries by CAPE and overweighting the cheapest 25% (≈ the 10 cheapest) historically beat buy-and-hold by a wide margin — but only as a basket, never one or two names.
ApplyYou already escape US home-bias by domicile. The discipline is to overweight the genuinely cheap (often unloved Europe/EM) and underweight the expensive (currently the US) — in a diversified basket, not a hero bet on Greece.
05The interesting information is in the tails
Buying countries at CAPE below 7 — at the worst possible headlines (Greece, Russia, Ireland) — historically returned ~14–30% CAGR; buying above 45 lost money for years. “Investing is the only business where, when things go on sale, people run out of the store.”
ApplyBe greedy when others flee — but in a basket, because a single falling-knife country can always halve again (“it went down 80%, then got cut in half”). Conviction and diversification, not one without the other.
06The absolute filter — cash as residue of opportunity
Faber's best variant only buys a market if it's below an absolute CAPE threshold; when nothing qualifies, that sleeve sits in cash. Lower drawdowns, similar or better returns.
ApplyThis is your diagnostic, mechanised. Cash because nothing is cheap is bottom-up and legitimate; cash because you forecast a crash is the top-down bet you've been told to resist. Faber draws the line for you.

Why Stock Markets Crash

Didier Sornette · 2003 · Princeton University Press
▾
The author

Didier Sornette is a physicist and geophysicist (ETH Zurich) who applies the mathematics of complex systems and critical phenomena — the same tools used for earthquakes and phase transitions — to financial markets. This is the most technically demanding book in the batch.

His thesis is that large crashes are not caused by the news of the day; they are endogenous critical events, the release of fragility that herding has been quietly accumulating for months or years. Intellectually rich; use it for the way of seeing, not as a trading oracle.

Principles & how to apply them
01Crashes as critical phenomena, not news events
A crash is a phase transition — the system reaches a critical point where a small perturbation triggers a cascade. The ‘cause’ everyone names afterward is incidental; the instability was already built in.
ApplyReframes “what caused the crash.” The fragility — leverage, crowding, stretched valuation — is the real story; the trigger is noise. Watch the build-up, not the headline.
02Log-periodic power-law signatures
Sornette finds bubbles often show accelerating, faster-than-exponential growth with speeding-up oscillations as they approach a critical point — a quantitative ‘fingerprint.’
ApplyFascinating, and worth understanding — but its out-of-sample record is mixed. Use it to sharpen your sense of fragility, never as a dated timing signal to go all-to-cash. That would be the very forecasting trap you're avoiding.
03Positive feedback and imitation
Herding and momentum create the self-reinforcing run-up: people buy because others are buying, until the move is unsustainable.
ApplyThe mechanism beneath Marks' pendulum and Faber's sentiment extremes. ‘Greed’ isn't a mood — it's a positive-feedback loop you can learn to recognise.
04It's the acceleration that warns, not the level
The danger sign is growth that is faster-than-exponential — unsustainable by definition — more than any particular price level.
ApplyA sober counter to ‘this time is different.’ When a story requires ever-accelerating belief to hold up, the fragility is near, whatever the absolute valuation.
05Prediction is probabilistic, never deterministic
Even Sornette's rigorous apparatus yields odds and ranges, not dates. He is explicit that this is risk assessment, not prophecy.
ApplyThe honest physicist's humility. The most quantitative crash model on your shelf still refuses to give you a date — which should permanently inoculate you against anyone who does.

The Black Swan

Nassim Nicholas Taleb · 2007 · the impact of the highly improbable
▾
The author

Nassim Taleb is a former options trader turned philosopher-essayist; the Incerto is his multi-book project on uncertainty. The Black Swan is its most famous instalment.

A ‘black swan’ is a rare, high-impact event that is rationalised only in hindsight. His charge: our models, built on the bell curve, are blind to exactly the events that dominate outcomes.

Principles & how to apply them
01Black swans dominate — and the Gaussian hides them
A handful of unpredictable events drive most of history and most of markets, yet the normal distribution assigns them near-zero probability. The map omits the territory that matters most.
ApplyNever size risk on models that assume thin tails. Your project-finance habit of stress-testing the downside case is the right instinct — extend it to the whole portfolio.
02The narrative fallacy and silent evidence
We invent tidy causal stories after the fact and never see the failures that didn't survive to be counted (survivorship bias).
ApplyDistrust the clean story explaining why a stock ‘had’ to rise, and distrust track records that only show the survivors — the direct extension of Fooled by Randomness.
03Mediocristan vs Extremistan

ApplyBecause one event can erase years, survival and convexity come before optimisation. Position sizing is a ruin-avoidance exercise, not a return-maximising one.
04The barbell
Combine extreme safety for most of your capital with small, capped bets that have explosive upside — rather than medium risk everywhere.
ApplyThis is your architecture: a large safe core plus a small high-conviction sleeve. Protect the base absolutely; take asymmetric shots with a slice you can afford to lose.
05Via negativa
Robustness comes more from removing fragilities than from adding cleverness — improvement by subtraction.
ApplyOften the best portfolio move is removing what could ruin you (leverage, concentration, a thesis you can't defend), not adding another clever position.

Antifragile

Nassim Nicholas Taleb · 2012 · things that gain from disorder
▾
The author

The centrepiece of the Incerto. Taleb coins antifragile for things that don't merely withstand disorder but actively benefit from it — a category he argues we have no word for and badly need.

Principles & how to apply them
01Fragile – robust – antifragile

ApplyRun the triage on every holding and rule: what shatters in a crash, what merely survives, and what — like dry powder plus a buy-list — actually gets stronger when prices fall?

▾
The authors

Jonathan Haskel is an economist at Imperial College London (later a member of the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee); Stian Westlake is a research and innovation-policy specialist. Principles & how to apply them
01Intangibles now dominate — and accounting can't see them

Three basic ideas before the regimes

Debt

A promise to pay money back later. It is dangerous when it grows faster than the income that must repay it.

Inflation and deflation

Rising prices shrink the real weight of a debt. Falling prices make the same debt heavier, which is why deflation is so dangerous for borrowers.

The printing question

A country that owes in its own currency can print its way out, at a cost. A country that owes in someone else’s currency cannot, so its currency becomes the battlefield.

Deflationary debt crisis

A credit bubble bursts in a country that owes its debt in its own currency. Everyone tries to repay at the same time, so spending, credit and prices shrink together.

Prints its currency: yesSpeed: sudden

What you would observe

  • Property and stock prices fall sharply.
  • Banks stop lending and credit spreads blow out.
  • Unemployment rises quickly, wages and prices stall or fall.
  • The central bank cuts rates toward zero, then creates money.

Why it happens

During the boom, debt grows faster than income, backed by rising asset prices. When prices stop rising, forced selling begins. Falling prices make debts heavier in real terms, which forces more selling: the debt-deflation spiral described by Irving Fisher in 1933. The exit exists because the central bank can print the currency the debt is in, and the government can spend to replace lost private demand.

Who gets hurt

Leveraged households, banks, highly indebted companies, and anyone forced to sell at the bottom.

Real cases

  • United States, 1929–1933. Prices fell by about a quarter, thousands of banks failed, a timid policy response turned a bust into a depression.
  • United States and United Kingdom, 2008–2009. A faster, bolder response: zero rates, quantitative easing and bank recapitalization produced what Dalio calls a beautiful deleveraging.

Held up

  • Cash and short-term government bills. Liquidity is king, and money gains purchasing power when prices fall.
  • Long-term high-quality government bonds. Rates collapse, so existing bonds paying higher coupons gain value. In 2008, long US Treasuries rose strongly while the S&P 500 lost 37%.
  • Businesses with strong balance sheets. They fall with the market but survive, and become the bargains of the bottom.

Suffered

  • Anything bought with borrowed money, property first.
  • Banks and financials, at the center of the bust.
  • Cyclical companies and commodities, as demand collapses.
  • High-yield corporate bonds, as defaults rise.

What it means for a private investor

This is the regime a cash reserve is built for. Keep dry powder in liquid, high-quality instruments, carry no leverage so you are never a forced seller, then buy quality businesses near the point of maximum pessimism, as Buffett did in 2008–2009.

Inflationary debt crisis

A country owes debt in a currency it cannot print, or its own savers stop trusting the local currency. Capital flees, the currency collapses and inflation jumps.

Prints its currency: no, or trust is lostSpeed: sudden

What you would observe

  • The currency falls sharply against the dollar and the euro.
  • Inflation spikes, starting with imported goods like fuel and medicines.
  • The central bank burns its reserves, then raises rates dramatically.
  • Locals convert savings into dollars or gold; capital controls or deposit freezes may follow.
  • It often ends with an IMF (International Monetary Fund) program.

Why it happens

Foreign capital financed the boom. When it reverses, sellers of the currency overwhelm the central bank. Foreign-currency debts grow heavier as the currency falls, and printing money to help only accelerates the fall, because the core problem is that nobody wants the money. Its extreme form is hyperinflation.

Who gets hurt

Savers holding local currency, companies with dollar debts and local revenues, importers, and governments with foreign-currency debt.

Real cases

  • Weimar Germany, 1921–1923. Hyperinflation wiped out middle-class savings.
  • Mexico 1994–1995, South Korea 1997–1998. Currency crises followed by export-led recoveries.
  • Argentina, 2001–2002 and repeatedly since.
  • Turkey, 2018–2024. Inflation peaked above 80% in 2022 as the lira kept sliding.
  • Lebanon, from 2019. Banks froze deposits and the currency lost more than 95% of its value.

Held up

  • Hard foreign currency, held abroad. It gains exactly what the local currency loses.
  • Gold. No government can print it and no counterparty can freeze it if held safely.
  • Real assets: property and productive farmland keep their real value over time.
  • Exporters and hard-currency earners. Their revenues rise in local terms when the currency falls.

Suffered

  • Local-currency cash and deposits, which melt.
  • Local-currency bonds, especially long maturities.
  • Regulated utilities, whose prices the state caps.
  • Importers and firms with dollar debt.

Afterward, a cheap currency makes the country competitive again. Patient investors who bought quality assets in South Korea after 1998 were rewarded.

What it means for a private investor

Own what has real or foreign-currency value, keep local-currency maturities short, and hold part of the safety net in another jurisdiction. Lebanon proved that where an asset sits legally matters as much as what the asset is.

Currency-union or peg crisis

A country tied to a shared or pegged currency can neither devalue nor print. The adjustment happens through falling wages, prices and output inside the country, or, if the peg breaks, through a sudden devaluation.

Prints its currency: noSpeed: sudden shock, grinding adjustment

What you would observe

  • Government bond spreads widen against the anchor country.
  • Deposits flee toward banks in stronger countries.
  • A doom loop between weak banks and a weak state, each dragging the other down.
  • Austerity, falling wages and mass unemployment: an “internal devaluation.”
  • Talk of bail-ins and break-up.

Why it happens

A strong shared currency brings cheap credit, which fuels imbalances. When the flow stops, the classic safety valve, a cheaper currency, does not exist. The lender of last resort sits outside the country, and its help comes with conditions.

Who gets hurt

Depositors above the guarantee, holders of local government bonds, local property owners, and workers in uncompetitive sectors.

Real cases

  • Greece, 2010–2015. The economy shrank by about a quarter.
  • Ireland, Portugal, Spain, 2010–2013. Bailouts and austerity, then recovery.
  • Cyprus, 2013. Deposits above €100,000 were partly converted into bank shares to rescue the banks.
  • Argentina, 2001–2002. The one-to-one dollar peg broke, deposits were frozen in the “corralito,” and the crisis flipped into the inflationary type.
  • France today sits structurally in this regime: under bond-market stress, not in crisis.

Held up

  • Anchor-country government bonds. German Bunds were the refuge in 2011–2012.
  • Globally diversified equities and bonds, whose value does not depend on one member state.
  • Deposits within the guarantee, spread across banks.
  • Gold, which rose strongly in euros during 2010–2012.

Suffered

  • Local bank shares and uninsured deposits.
  • Local government bonds.
  • Local property and domestically focused companies.

Afterward, crisis-country assets became deeply cheap. Investors who bought Irish, Spanish or Greek assets near the bottom, with patience, did very well.

What it means for a private investor

Diversify outside the country even inside the union, respect deposit guarantees bank by bank, and avoid stacking your job, home, savings and bond holdings on the same state.

Japanification

The slow-motion deflationary regime. No single crash after the bubble, but a decade or more of debt repayment, zombie banks, and near-zero growth and inflation.

Prints its currency: yesSpeed: slow

What you would observe

  • Interest rates near zero for years.
  • Flat or slowly falling prices.
  • Property and stock prices drifting lower for a decade or more.
  • Companies hoarding cash and repaying debt; weak demand for credit despite cheap money.

Why it happens

After the bubble, companies focus on repairing their balance sheets instead of maximizing profits, a “balance sheet recession” in economist Richard Koo’s words. Banks avoid recognizing losses by rolling over loans to weak borrowers. Monetary stimulus pushes on a string, and an ageing population often deepens the stagnation.

Who gets hurt

Domestic property owners, domestic stock investors, banks, and savers who need income.

Real cases

  • Japan, 1990–2012. The Nikkei 225 peaked at the end of 1989 and only regained that level in 2024. Big-city property fell by well over half.
  • Eurozone, 2012–2020. Partial symptoms: negative rates and weak growth.

Held up

  • Cash. With prices drifting down, its real value held or rose.
  • Long government bonds. Japanese 10-year yields fell from around 7% in 1990 to below 1%, delivering large price gains.
  • Global assets, which did far better than domestic ones.
  • Companies with strong balance sheets and global revenues.

Suffered

  • Domestic property.
  • Broad domestic equities.
  • Banks.
  • Anything relying on rising prices or leverage.

What it means for a private investor

The lesson is home bias: a bet on your own country can stay dead for a generation. Global diversification and patience with income-producing assets are the protection.

Financial repression

The slow-motion inflationary exit. Interest rates are held below inflation for years so that public debt melts in real terms, and savers pay a silent tax.

Prints its currency: yesSpeed: slow

What you would observe

  • Interest rates persistently below inflation: negative real rates.
  • Rules nudging banks, insurers and pension funds to hold government bonds.
  • The central bank owning a large share of public debt.
  • Debt-to-GDP (gross domestic product) falling without visible austerity.

Why it happens

It is the politically least painful way to shrink a debt: no default, no headline tax increase. Dalio considers it the most likely path for today’s heavily indebted rich countries.

Who gets hurt

Savers in deposits, holders of long fixed-rate bonds, and anyone living on fixed annuities or fixed pensions.

Real cases

  • United States, 1945–1980. Federal debt fell from above 100% of GDP to about a third of it.
  • United Kingdom, 1945–1980. Debt fell from around 250% of GDP to roughly 50%, largely at the expense of bondholders.
  • Eurozone and Japan, mid-2010s to early 2020s. Negative policy rates and central bank control of bond yields.

Held up

  • Businesses with pricing power, which pass inflation on to customers.
  • Real estate, especially with long fixed-rate debt. Inflation shrinks the loan while the asset reprices.
  • Inflation-linked bonds, where available.
  • Gold, notably in the 1970s phase.

Suffered

  • Savings accounts and cash.
  • Long fixed-rate government bonds. British gilt holders lost much of their purchasing power.
  • Fixed annuities.

What it means for a private investor

In this regime a long fixed-rate loan becomes an asset, because inflation shrinks what you owe. Core holdings should be productive assets that reprice with inflation; fixed income should stay short.

Stagflation

Prices rise while growth stalls and unemployment climbs, usually after a supply shock such as energy. It can sit on top of any debt regime and makes every policy choice harder.

Prints its currency: variesSpeed: slow, often a decade

What you would observe

  • High inflation alongside weak or negative growth.
  • Energy and commodity price spikes.
  • A central bank caught between deepening the recession and feeding inflation.
  • Stocks and bonds falling at the same time; valuation multiples compressing.

Why it happens

A supply shock raises costs everywhere at once. Wages chase prices and inflation expectations come loose. Outcomes then hinge on the central bank: if it stays behind the curve, real rates are negative and real assets shine; if it fights hard, as Paul Volcker did in 1980–1982, real rates turn positive and even gold falls.

Who gets hurt

Holders of long-duration assets (growth stocks, long bonds), consumers, energy-intensive industries, and companies without pricing power.

Real cases

  • United States and United Kingdom, 1973–1982. Two oil shocks; US inflation near 15% in 1980, UK inflation above 20% in 1975. US stocks lost value in real terms from 1966 to 1982.
  • 2022. An energy shock in which stocks and bonds fell together.
  • Today’s energy shock carries elements of this regime.

Held up

  • Energy and commodity producers, selling what is scarce.
  • Short-term bills, whose rates reset quickly.
  • Inflation-linked bonds.
  • Value businesses with pricing power and low capital needs, the profile Buffett described in his 1977 essay on inflation.
  • Gold, conditionally: only while real rates stay negative.

Suffered

  • Long fixed-rate bonds.
  • Growth stocks valued on distant profits.
  • Broad equities in real terms.
  • Consumer discretionary businesses.

What it means for a private investor

Pricing power beats sector labels. Test every “inflation hedge” for capital intensity: a company that must rebuild plants at inflated costs runs up a down escalator. Treat gold as a conditional hedge that works only if the central bank blinks.

Sovereign default

A government stops paying or forces its lenders to accept less. It can happen inside any regime above, most often where the state cannot print what it owes.

Prints its currency: usually noSpeed: sudden event after a slow build-up

What you would observe

  • Government bond prices collapse; ratings fall to default.
  • Domestic banks, which hold the state’s debt, come under stress.
  • Deposit freezes or capital controls become possible.
  • Negotiations with creditors, often an IMF program.

Why it happens

Debt service exceeds what the state can raise through taxes or new borrowing. When market access closes and no lender of last resort steps in, restructuring is the only exit.

Who gets hurt

Holders of the government’s bonds, domestic depositors and bank shareholders, and pensioners whose funds hold state debt.

Real cases

  • Russia, 1998. Default combined with a rouble devaluation.
  • Argentina, 2001 and again 2020.
  • Greece, 2012. Private bondholders lost roughly half the face value of their bonds.
  • Lebanon, 2020, and Sri Lanka, 2022.

Held up

  • Foreign currency and assets held abroad, beyond the reach of local controls.
  • Gold.
  • Real assets, which keep value even if illiquid for a while.
  • Exporters, earning in hard currency.

Suffered

  • The government’s own bonds.
  • Domestic banks, hit at the same time as the state.
  • Local-currency savings and domestic financial assets.

Afterward, defaulted bonds sometimes trade at very deep discounts. That is specialist territory, where serious credit-analysis skills can become an edge.

What it means for a private investor

Separate where you earn from where you store. Never concentrate your safety net inside a state and a banking system exposed to the same risk.

The real lesson: you don’t need to guess the regime

Read the matrix row by row. Three categories are never marked “suffered,” in any regime. Two are marked “suffered” almost everywhere. That pattern is the whole strategy.

Never in the “suffered” column

Quality businesses with pricing power and strong balance sheets. Assets diversified across countries and currencies, held safely outside any single troubled system. And gold, which produces nothing, so it belongs as capped insurance, never as the engine.

Almost always in the “suffered” column

Long-term fixed-rate bonds in a weakening currency, and concentration in one country’s banks, bonds and property. Add borrowed money, and any regime becomes fatal.

Why Dalio and Buffett converge

Dalio’s All Weather says: balance across regimes because you cannot forecast. Buffett says: own businesses that can raise prices without heavy reinvestment. Both arrive at a portfolio no single regime can wound mortally.
