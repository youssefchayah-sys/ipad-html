The Table · À Table

The gastronomy — a cheese map you can eat

Lac Léman is a rare border where two great cheese cultures press against each other across the water: the Swiss alpine tradition to the north and east, the Savoyard to the south. Learn the handful of names below and you'll read any menu on either shore like a local.

🇨🇭 Swiss cheeses — the essentials

The Alpine canon

  • Gruyère AOP — the king; nutty, from the town of Gruyères (castle + Maison du Gruyère visitable).
  • Vacherin Mont d'Or AOP — winter-only, boxed in spruce; bake it whole (boîte chaude) and spoon it out.
  • L'Etivaz AOP — Gruyère's wilder mountain cousin, made over wood fire.
  • Tête de Moine — shaved into flower-like curls with a girolle.
  • Tomme Vaudoise · Raclette du Valais AOP · Emmentaler.
🇫🇷 Savoyard cheeses — the essentials

The Haute-Savoie canon

  • Reblochon AOP — the soul of tartiflette; from the Aravis, born of a tax dodge (see Dinner Lines).
  • Abondance AOP — from the valley just above your lake; the cheese behind berthoud.
  • Beaufort AOP — "the prince of Gruyères," firm and floral.
  • Tomme de Savoie IGP · Chevrotin AOP · Raclette de Savoie.
🍽️ The dishes to order lakeside

What to actually eat

  • Filets de perche du Léman — the lakeside dish; perch fillets, meunière, with a Chasselas. (Ask du lac — much sold is imported.)
  • Fondue moitié-moitié (CH: half Gruyère, half Vacherin Fribourgeois) vs fondue savoyarde (FR: Beaufort/Comté/Emmental).
  • Tartiflette, berthoud, papet vaudois, malakoffs (Vaud cheese fritters), longeole (Geneva sausage), diots & crozets (Savoie).
🍷 The wines — one grape rules

Chasselas country

  • Lavaux grands crus (Dézaley, Saint-Saphorin, Epesses) — Switzerland's finest whites, on UNESCO terraces.
  • La Côte (Nyon–Morges) and Geneva wines.
  • French shore: Crépy, Marin, Marignan, Ripaille — the Savoyard Chasselas AOCs.
🍫 The sweet side

Chocolate & more

  • Vevey is the birthplace of milk chocolate (Nestlé + Daniel Peter); Cailler at nearby Broc runs a factory visit.
  • Geneva's pavés and marzipan marmite de l'Escalade (December).
👶 With Emeer & Eline

Family-easy tastes

  • Friture du lac and rösti please small palates; chocolate factory visits are a toddler win.
  • Raclette/tartiflette nights travel well; the bresaola lesson applies here too — keep a snack in the bag.
The Set-Pieces

Scenery & history — the big rocks

Beyond the villages, a handful of landmarks anchor the whole region. Hit these and you have the spine of the story.

The icon

Château de Chillon

Switzerland's most-visited monument — a fairy-tale fortress rising straight out of the water near Montreux. Lord Byron's The Prisoner of Chillon made it world-famous (his name is carved in the dungeon). A near-perfect outing with a small child's imagination.

UNESCO

Lavaux vineyard terraces

Stone-walled vines tumbling from Lausanne to Montreux, worked since the 11th century by monks. The locals say the grapes get three suns: the sky, the reflection off the lake, and the heat stored in the walls. Walk the terraces above Saint-Saphorin.

Your balcony

Mont Salève

"Geneva's balcony" — a cable car minutes from Annemasse lifts you to a cliff-top panorama of the whole lake, the city and Mont Blanc. The single easiest big-view win from your front door.

Belle Époque

The CGN paddle steamers

The largest fleet of restored early-1900s steamships in the world still crosses the lake. Board one at Nyon, Lausanne or Évian and glide between countries the way the Belle Époque did.

Culture

Vevey & Lausanne

Chaplin's World in Vevey (his final home) and the giant Fork in the lake; Lausanne's Olympic Museum (the IOC's home city) and its Gothic cathedral. Rainy-day insurance with real depth.

Châteaux & water

Ripaille, Coppet, Nyon, Prangins

A necklace of lakefront castles: Château de Ripaille (Thonon, with its own vineyard), Coppet (Madame de Staël's salon), Nyon (Roman + five white towers), Prangins (the national museum).

The Confederation · Die Kantone

The twenty-six cantons — the board behind the lake

Your lake touches three of them. The other twenty-three are the rest of the country, and each one is a small state with its own tax code, school system, holidays, accent and reputation. Learn the emblem, the cliché and the one line for each, and you can hold any Swiss table — because the Swiss themselves run on exactly this material.

The structure

26 — but officially 20 ½

Twenty full cantons and six “halves” (BS, BL, OW, NW, AR, AI), each with half a vote in the upper house. A 19th-century truce that nobody has dared reopen.

The fault lines

The Röstigraben

The invisible ditch between French and German Switzerland, running roughly along the Sarine — through the canton and the city of Fribourg. South of the Gotthard, the same joke becomes the Polentagraben.

The temperament

Kantönligeist

“Little-canton spirit”: the parochial pride that keeps 26 school systems and 26 police forces running in a country smaller than Aquitaine. It is a criticism and a compliment at once.

Italiano joined 1803 🚗 ~4h30
The emblemPalm trees and Italian light: Lugano and Locarno, three UNESCO castles at Bellinzona, grotti under chestnut trees, risotto, polenta and Merlot.
The clichéThe sunny corner — warm, talkative, expressive, and convinced that everyone « north of the Gotthard » forgets it exists. Usually right about that.
The lineTicino was a conquered subject territory, not a partner: the Swiss ruled it as a colony for three centuries. It became an equal canton only in 1803, under a constitution imposed by Napoleon, with the motto « liberi e svizzeri » — free and Swiss. Its signature wine is Merlot, a Bordeaux grape planted after phylloxera wiped out everything else.

Ten confederation lines

Pan-Swiss · surprising · table-ready
  1. Switzerland has 26 cantons — but officially 20 full ones and six halves, a 19th-century compromise that still gives Basel-Stadt, Basel-Landschaft, Obwalden, Nidwalden and the two Appenzells half a vote each in the upper house.
  2. The country is named after Schwyz, a canton of 160,000 people you have probably never visited — and the Swiss flag is that canton's banner.
  3. Switzerland has no capital city in its constitution. Bern is only the « federal city », a deliberate 1848 fudge so that no canton could claim the crown.
  4. The Röstigraben — the « rösti ditch » — is the invisible line between French- and German-speaking Switzerland. It runs roughly along the Sarine/Saane, straight through the canton of Fribourg and its capital. South of the Gotthard, the equivalent joke is the Polentagraben.
  5. Two cantons still govern by open-air assembly: Glarus and Appenzell Innerrhoden gather every citizen in a square once a year to vote by raised hand. In Appenzell, men carry a ceremonial sword as proof of eligibility.
  6. Appenzell Innerrhoden gave women the cantonal vote only in 1990 — the last jurisdiction in the Western world — and only after the Federal Court forced it.
  7. Jura, the youngest canton, is younger than most people at your table: created in 1979 after a separatist fight with Bern. The town of Moutier voted to switch sides only in 2021.
  8. Swiss number-plate letters are a national sport. ZH is read as « z'wenig Hirn » — not enough brain — and AG as « Achtung Gefahr », danger ahead.
  9. « Kantönligeist » — little-canton spirit — is the Swiss word for the parochial pride that keeps 26 separate school systems, tax codes and police forces running in a country smaller than Aquitaine.
  10. Your lake touches only three of the 26: Geneva, Vaud and Valais. Cross the water at Saint-Gingolph and you change country; drive to Le Bouveret and you change canton — a different tax code, a different school system, a different set of public holidays, all within a lunch-and-back radius of Annemasse.

Dinner lines — facts worth carrying

Ten things to drop at a table

Surprising · specific · shareable
  1. Saint-Gingolph is a single village split between two countries — the France–Switzerland border runs down a stream through the middle, so you can cross nations on a footbridge over a creek. It was, unsurprisingly, prime smuggling country.
  2. Excenevex has the only natural sandy beach on the whole of Lake Geneva — everywhere else is pebbles or built-up quay.
  3. Reblochon, the cheese in tartiflette, was born as a tax dodge: Savoyard farmers under-milked their cows for the landlord's count, then did a second 'rebloche' (re-pinch) afterwards for a rich, illicit second milk.
  4. The Lavaux winemakers say their grapes get three suns — the sky, the reflection off the lake, and the heat radiating out of the stone terrace walls. The vines have been worked since medieval monks built them.
  5. Milk chocolate as we know it was invented in Vevey, on this lake, when Daniel Peter figured out how to blend Henri Nestlé's condensed milk into it.
  6. Vacherin Mont d'Or is a cheese you can only buy in winter — it's aged in a spruce-bark box, and the classic move is to bake the whole thing and eat it molten with a spoon.
  7. Château de Chillon is Switzerland's most-visited historic building, and Lord Byron scratched his name into a dungeon pillar there in 1816 — the graffiti is now a protected exhibit.
  8. The 'perche du lac' on a lakeside menu is often not from the lake at all — genuine Léman perch is scarce and pricey, so ask specifically, and treat the real thing as the delicacy it is.
  9. Lake Geneva runs the largest fleet of restored Belle Époque paddle steamers in the world — you can still commute between France and Switzerland on a coal-era steamship.
  10. Mont Salève, the cliff looming over Geneva, is entirely in France — the Genevois nickname it 'their' mountain and take a cable car up it, but they need a passport to reach the top.
