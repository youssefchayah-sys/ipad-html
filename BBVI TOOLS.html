ENGINE 1 — The Quality Engine · "Is it a wonderful business?"

Every ratio below is built from the financial statements alone. It measures the business — never the price. Read the LEVEL (how good) and, above all, the TREND over 5–10 years (stable = moat; eroding = moat dissolving).

ENGINE 2 — The Price / Value Engine · "Is it selling for less than it's worth?"

These need the market price (MKT). Quality tells you WHAT to want; valuation tells you WHAT to pay. A wonderful business at a terrible price still violates Rule #1.

THE BRIDGE — Justified Multiples & Value Drivers · converting Quality into deserved Price

These are the gears that mesh the two engines. They take the Quality output (ROE / ROIC / growth) and compute the multiple — the value — that quality DESERVES. The gap vs. the market multiple IS your margin of safety.

How the Three Valuation Models Articulate — one equation, three rotations

The Value Driver Model, the Justified Multiple, and the Total Return Decomposition are not three tools — they are one equation seen from three angles. The shared root is the Gordon / DCF perpetuity:

Value = Cash Flow ÷ ( r − g )
① ABSOLUTE — "What is it worth?"

Value Driver Model.
Value = NOPAT × (1 − g/ROIC) ÷ (WACC − g).
The perpetuity in euros. The parent form → gives an intrinsic value to compare vs. market cap. Use for a full DCF verdict.

② RATIO — "What multiple does it deserve?"

Justified Multiple.
Divide ① by a fundamental → the euros cancel into a ratio:
P/B = (ROE − g)/(r − g); EV/IC = (ROIC − g)/(WACC − g). Use for a fast relative check vs. the market multiple.

③ RATE — "What will I earn?"

Total Return Decomposition.
Solve ① for r:  r = (D₁/P) + g
= Dividend Yield + Growth ± Δ Multiple. Use to forecast forward return and see its sources.

THE CLOSURE:  buy at the justified multiple (price = fair value) → the Δ-multiple term = 0 → your return = Dividend Yield + Growth = r, the very discount rate used in ①. Pay fair value, earn exactly the cost of equity. The three must agree — they are the same equation.

One engine, three dials: choose whether to read the output as a price (①), a multiple (②), or a return (③). Underwrite the yield + growth you can defend; refuse to pay for the speculative re-rating you cannot.

SYNTHESIS — The Two Driver Engines, Fused

Engine 1 · The DuPont Quality Engine — decomposing ROE into its drivers

3-STEP:  ROE = Net Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier
           = (NI/Revenue) × (Revenue/Assets) × (Assets/Equity)
5-STEP:  ROE = Tax Burden × Interest Burden × Operating Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier
           = (NI/EBT)×(EBT/EBIT)×(EBIT/Rev)×(Rev/Assets)×(Assets/Equity)

What it does: shatters a single ROE into profitability × efficiency × leverage — and the 5-step further splits profitability into operating skill vs. financing vs. tax. It is the lie-detector for ROE: it forces the number to confess whether the return is earned (margin/turnover) or merely borrowed (equity multiplier).

Engine 2 · The Value-Driver Engine — decomposing intrinsic value into its drivers

Value = NOPAT × ( 1 − g / ROIC ) ÷ ( WACC − g )

What it does: factors a company's entire worth into three drivers — ROIC (quality), g (growth), WACC (cost of capital). The term (1 − g/ROIC) delivers the verdict of value investing: growth creates value only when ROIC > WACC; below it, growth destroys value. Quality is the gatekeeper; growth is worth paying for only once quality clears the cost of capital.

The Gear That Meshes Them — the Justified Multiple

Justified P/B = ( ROE − g ) ÷ ( r − g )      Justified EV/IC = ( ROIC − g ) ÷ ( WACC − g )

The justified multiple takes the output of the Quality engine (ROE/ROIC) and turns it into the input of the Price engine (the deserved multiple). That is the seam where the two machines lock together.

The Buffett Intersection. He does not buy high-quality businesses (Munger's half) or cheap multiples (Graham's half) — he buys high-ROIC businesses trading below their justified multiple. Quality confirmed by DuPont; price confirmed with a margin of safety by the justified multiple. That single intersection — wonderful business AND cheap — is the only place a euro should move.
The discipline in one line. Quality without price is a wait. Price without quality is a trap. Only quality + price + margin of safety is a buy. This model measures quality brilliantly — never let it move you without the price verdict beside it.

WHEN TO USE EACH RATIO · matching the lens to the question & the business

No ratio is "best" — each is a lens for a different question and a different business type. The art is picking the lens that could falsify your thesis, not the one that flatters it. Then triangulate — the divergences between ratios are where the truth hides.

Three deadly traps. (1) Equity vs Enterprise: when leverage varies across your comparison set, use EV multiples (they neutralize capital structure) — use equity multiples for financials. (2) Cyclical inversion: for energy/commodities/autos, a LOW P/E usually means PEAK earnings (a sell signal) and a high/negative P/E means trough (the buy) — the P/E inverts; use normalized earnings or P/B. (3) A multiple alone lies: a low multiple on collapsing ROIC/margins is a value trap; a high multiple on a durable high-ROIC compounder can be a bargain — never read a price ratio without the quality verdict beside it.

APPLYING THE FRAMEWORK TO ETFs · a basket in a wrapper, not a business

An ETF has no operations, no moat, no COGS — so the engines don't apply to the ETF directly. They apply in THREE layers: the basket it holds, the structure that wraps it, and the valuation you buy it at. Run all three.

The core reframing. A single stock demands you judge a business. An ETF demands you judge a basket + a structure + a starting valuation. The two engines still run on the basket (Layer 1) — but you bolt on a wrapper checklist (Layer 2), and for broad-market funds your edge migrates from stock selection to cost minimization and valuation-based entry timing (Layer 3).

Layer 1 · Look-Through Fundamentals — run both engines on the weighted basket ↑ top

You can't compute ROIC on the wrapper — but you can on the weighted-average of everything it holds. Providers publish these "portfolio characteristics." This is where the entire Quality + Price framework transfers intact.

Layer 3 · Index-Level Expected Return — the master lens for a whole basket ↑ top

The Total Return Decomposition (Bogle) was invented FOR indices — idiosyncratic noise averages out, so it's more reliable here than for a single stock. CAPE is its valuation input.

ToolCategoryFormulaUsesWhy / what it reveals
Index Total Return DecompositionExpected returnDividend Yield + Earnings Growth ± Δ MultipleMKTISThe Bogle lens — splits an index's forward return into fundamental (yield + growth) vs. speculative (re-rating). Underwrite the fundamental; don't pay for the re-rating.
CAPE (Shiller P/E)Index valuationPrice ÷ 10-yr average real earningsMKTISJustified-multiple logic for a whole index. High CAPE → low forward returns (multiple must compress); low CAPE → high forward returns. Your entry-timing gauge.

What does NOT transfer. With a broad ETF you can't demand a stock-picker's margin of safety on individual mispricing — you're buying the market's average quality at the market's price. So the value discipline SHIFTS: from "find the mispriced company" to (1) minimize cost, (2) time entry via index valuation (CAPE), (3) refuse to buy when the return is mostly borrowed from multiple expansion. For a cash-and-wait investor, CAPE + the return decomposition ARE your margin of safety — deploy tranches when the multiple compresses and forward-return math turns in your favour, not on a hunch.

FOUNDATIONS & CONCEPTS · everything else we built, in one map

The market-structure and accounting concepts that sit underneath the ratios — who's who in the financial system, and what the balance-sheet building blocks actually mean.

Market Structure — the map of the financial system ↑ top

ConceptKey distinction / what it is
Sell-side vs Buy-sideSell-side = investment banks that create & sell securities and services (the shipyard). Buy-side = institutions that own capital and deploy it — the ones who write the cheques (pensions, funds, PE, family offices). You, as an investor, are buy-side.
Investment Banking: Industry vs Product GroupIndustry Group (coverage) = organised by who the client is (Healthcare, Energy…) — owns the relationship, originates deals. Product Group = organised by what the deal is (M&A, DCM, ECM) — the technical specialists who execute. Coverage opens the door; product delivers the transaction.
Primary vs Secondary MarketPrimary = a brand-new security is created (IPO, new bond issue — the builder selling new). Secondary = existing securities change hands (the resale market). Defined by the ACTIVITY (creation vs resale), not the division. "Capital Markets" contains BOTH.
SyndicationSplitting a deal too big for one bank across a group (syndicate) — spreads the risk and fees. The backbone of large loans & project finance.
Securitization → ABS / MBSThe factory that bundles illiquid loans into tradable bonds. ABS = backed by general loans (auto, cards); MBS = backed by mortgages. A primary-market (creation) activity that also trades secondary.
Buy-side: Traditional vs Non-TraditionalTraditional = long-only, liquid, regulated (pensions, insurers, endowments, mutual funds). Non-Traditional / alternatives = go-anywhere, illiquid, lightly regulated (hedge funds, PE, VC, distressed). Family offices straddle both — permanent capital, total freedom.

Feast times: HF any, PE cheap-money booms, distressed recessions.

Family OfficeManages one family's fortune — no outside investors, so the MOST flexible vehicle (permanent capital, no redemptions). The structural edge YOU replicate as an individual: nobody can force you to sell. (Archegos = its danger without discipline.)

IG vs HY bondsInvestment Grade (BBB−/Baa3+) = strong issuers, low default risk, lower yield. High Yield / junk (BB+/Ba1−) = weaker issuers, higher default risk, higher yield. HY is the feeder pond for distressed funds.

Accounting Building Blocks ↑ top

ConceptKey distinction / what it is
The 3 statementsIncome Statement = profit flow over a year. Balance Sheet = snapshot of assets/debt/equity on one day. Cash Flow = real cash in/out (the truth serum). Profit is opinion; cash is fact.
The interest line = operating vs financing divideAbove it (EBIT, NOPAT) = profit belonging to ALL capital providers, before the split. Below it (Net Income) = the shareholders' slice, after debtholders (interest) & government (tax) are paid.
EBIT & NOPATEBIT = operating profit, the pool before it's split. NOPAT = EBIT × (1 − tax rate) = after-tax operating profit as if debt-free (financing-neutral). = Net Income + after-tax interest. The correct numerator for ROIC.
Interest tax shield (why NOPAT taxes EBIT)NOPAT taxes EBIT (not EBT) to simulate an unlevered firm — deliberately IGNORING the tax benefit of debt. That shield is instead counted in WACC's after-tax cost of debt. Counted once, not twice — prevents double-counting.
COGS vs SG&A vs OtherCOGS = direct product costs (variable, scale with units). SG&A = operating overhead (semi-fixed, cost of the machine). Gross Profit strips only COGS; Operating Profit strips COGS + SG&A + D&A.
PP&EProperty, Plant & Equipment — long-term physical operating assets (stores, plants, machines), shown net of accumulated depreciation. The long-term half of operating capital.
Gross vs Net DebtGross = all interest-bearing borrowings. Net = gross − cash (the true economic debt). Negative net debt = net-cash fortress. Only interest-bearing counts (payables are operating, not debt).
Common Equity vs Retained EarningsCommon Equity = capital owners paid IN. Retained Earnings = cumulative profits kept & reinvested. Together = shareholders' equity. Large retained earnings = a self-funding compounding machine.
Free Supplier Financing (payables) & AccrualsPayables = buying now, paying suppliers later — an interest-free loan (trade credit). Accruals = expenses incurred but not yet paid (wages, taxes, interest owed). Both are free operating financing → subtracted from operating capital.
Accruals (quality sense)Accruals = Net Income − Operating Cash Flow — the gap between paper profit & real cash. HIGH accruals = LOW earnings quality (a red flag the profit isn't turning to cash).
Free Cash FlowSimple: CFO − CapEx. Built up (FCFF) = NOPAT + D&A − CapEx − ΔWorking Capital (to all capital). FCFE = to equity only. Owner Earnings (Buffett) subtracts only MAINTENANCE capex. The DCF runs on this.
Working Capital & Cash Conversion CycleNWC = (inventory + receivables) − payables. CCC = DSO + DIO − DPO = days cash is trapped. Negative CCC (Walmart) = suppliers & customers finance you = a hidden moat / float.
WACC(E/V × cost of equity) + (D/V × after-tax cost of debt). Cost of equity via CAPM = risk-free + β × equity risk premium. The hurdle rate ROIC must beat. An estimate, not a fact — hold it as a range.

Capital Concepts — reconciled ↑ top

ConceptKey distinction / what it is
Net Assets vs Invested CapitalSAME number, two sides of the balance sheet. Net Assets = asset side (PP&E + Net Working Capital) — "where capital sits." Invested Capital = financing side (Net Debt + Equity) — "where capital came from." Equal by the balance-sheet identity.
Net Operating Assets vs Invested CapitalEqual for a pure operating business. Diverge with non-operating assets: Invested Capital = NOA + Non-Operating Assets. NOA = operating engine only; IC also funds any investment portfolio (holdcos like Exor).
RONA vs ROICRONA = operational lens (profit ÷ operating net assets — how well operators use their assets). ROIC = investor lens (NOPAT ÷ invested capital — return vs WACC). Same for pure operators; use RONA to isolate the operating engine, ROIC for the value-creation verdict.
Why subtract cash (net) & current liabilitiesStrip cash because idle cash isn't deployed in operations. Strip operating current liabilities (payables/accruals) because they're free financing investors never provided. What's left = capital actually at work → matches the operating-profit numerator.
Sum-of-the-Parts (holdcos)For Exor/Berkshire/Investor AB: never a blended ROIC. Value the operating stub on its own NOA + the investment portfolio at market → compare to price (discount-to-NAV).

Macro & Behavioural Lenses ↑ top

ConceptKey distinction / what it is
Long-Term Economic Outlook (LTEO)Multi-year forecast of growth, inflation, rates. Sets fixed-income positioning: duration (short if rates rising, long if falling) & credit stance (IG if recession looms, HY if growth strong) BEFORE screening bonds. The disciplined way to use macro without becoming a macro trader.
Stock-Bond Correlation (regime-dependent)NOT a law. Negative when growth shocks dominate (recession → bonds hedge stocks, the 60/40 golden age). Positive when inflation dominates (stagflation → both fall together, e.g. 2022). Your stagflation view = the regime where the classic bond hedge FAILS → real assets hedge instead.
CAPE (Shiller P/E)Price ÷ 10-yr average real earnings — the justified-multiple logic for a whole index. High CAPE → low forward returns; low CAPE → high. Your entry-timing gauge for deploying cash tranches.
Dividend Yield & the Yield TrapDPS ÷ Price. Because price is the denominator, a HIGH yield can signal a COLLAPSING stock (dividend about to be cut), not a bargain. Always check payout coverage. Yield is one leg of total return — a growing dividend beats a fat static one.
Cash as Optionality (behavioural)Holding cash is a POSITION, not the absence of one — a no-expiry call on every asset. The discipline of waiting is an edge (permanent capital), but its carrying cost is inflation. Deploy on valuation math (CAPE), not a hunch — that's margin of safety, not market timing.

CALCULATION DEEP-DIVES · the worked math behind the ratios

The step-by-step derivations we worked through — not just the formula, but WHY it's built that way, with worked numbers (mostly from the Walmart model). Acronyms: NOPAT = Net Operating Profit After Tax · EBIT = Earnings Before Interest & Taxes · EBT = Earnings Before Tax · FCF = Free Cash Flow · CFO = Cash Flow from Operations · CapEx = Capital Expenditure · NWC = Net Working Capital · WACC = Weighted Average Cost of Capital.

1 · NOPAT — and why it taxes EBIT, not EBT ↑ top

NOPAT = EBIT × ( 1 − effective tax rate )   where effective tax rate = Tax ÷ EBT

Worked (Walmart yr 1): EBIT 26,696; effective tax rate ≈ 32.8% → NOPAT = 26,696 × (1 − 0.328) = 17,940. Compare Net Income 16,363 — NOPAT is higher by ~1,577 = the after-tax interest added back.

Why tax EBIT, not EBT? NOPAT simulates an unlevered (debt-free) firm: with no debt there's no interest, so EBT would equal EBIT and tax would fall on EBIT. This deliberately IGNORES the interest tax shield (the tax saving from deductible interest) — because that shield is a FINANCING effect, counted instead in WACC's after-tax cost of debt. Counted once, never twice. Equivalent identity: NOPAT = Net Income + after-tax interest expense. It's the correct numerator for ROIC because it matches ROIC's all-capital denominator (Net Debt + Equity).

2 · Invested Capital = Net Assets — the two-sided proof ↑ top

Start: Total Assets = Total Liabilities + Equity
Cash + OtherCurrentAssets + PP&E = Payables + Debt + Equity
— subtract Payables & Cash from both sides —
(OtherCA + PP&E − Payables) − Cash  =  (Debt − Cash) + Equity
NET ASSETS (asset side)  =  INVESTED CAPITAL (financing side)

Worked (Walmart yr 1): Invested Capital = Net Debt 54,594 + Equity 81,394 = 135,988. Net Assets = PP&E 140,428 + Net Working Capital (−4,440) = 135,988. Equal by the balance-sheet identity — same capital, asset side vs financing side. Diverges only by the value of non-operating assets: Invested Capital = Net Operating Assets + Non-Operating Assets (matters for holdcos like Exor).

3 · Free Cash Flow — the four routes ↑ top

Simple:  FCF = CFO − CapEx
Build-up (to firm, FCFF):  NOPAT + D&A − CapEx − ΔNet Working Capital
To equity (FCFE):  FCFF − after-tax interest + net borrowing  (= CFO − CapEx − net debt repaid)
Owner Earnings (Buffett):  Net Income + D&A − maintenance CapEx ± ΔWorking Capital

The key nuance — maintenance vs growth CapEx. Total CapEx bundles two things: maintenance/sustaining capex (replacing worn-out assets to hold current capacity — a real, unavoidable cost, capitalized) and growth capex (expansion — discretionary). Owner Earnings subtracts only MAINTENANCE capex to reveal true distributable earning power. (Note: routine repairs & maintenance are OpEx, expensed — a different thing from "maintenance capex," which is replacement CAPEX.) Rough proxy: maintenance capex ≈ depreciation. FCFF pairs with ROIC/EV; FCFE pairs with P/E. The DCF discounts future FCF — this is the number intrinsic value runs on.

4 · Incremental ROIC — return on NEW capital, with the lag ↑ top

Incremental ROIC = Δ NOPAT ÷ Δ Invested Capital  (measure over a 3–5 yr span, not one year)
Compounding identity:  Growth = Reinvestment Rate × Incremental ROIC

Two traps. (1) Noise — single years explode (near-zero ΔIC denominator); use a multi-year endpoint-to-endpoint span. (2) The lag — newly-deployed capital doesn't earn its return until it's built & ramped, so pair this period's ΔNOPAT with Invested Capital deployed 1–3 years EARLIER (match today's harvest to yesterday's planting). Lag length = build time + ramp: software/retail ~0–1 yr; industrials ~1–2 yr; utilities/energy/infrastructure ~3–5 yr. The verdict: compare incremental ROIC to base ROIC — if incremental ≪ base, the compounder is stalling (past glory, dim future); high & durable incremental ROIC = a long reinvestment runway.

The Reinvestment Rate (in Growth = Reinvestment Rate × Return). Two forms, match the level: Firm (with ROIC): (Net CapEx + ΔNWC) ÷ NOPAT, where Net CapEx = CapEx − Depreciation — the share of operating profit plowed back into operating assets. Equity (with ROE): the retention ratio = 1 − Payout Ratio = retained earnings ÷ net income. Worked: NOPAT 100, CapEx 30, Dep 18 → Net CapEx 12; ΔNWC +8 → reinvestment 20 → rate 20%. If ROIC 15% → self-funded growth = 20% × 15% = 3%. Grow faster than this and the company must raise external capital (debt or dilution).

5 · WACC — the build-up ↑ top

WACC = (E/V × Rₑ) + (D/V × R_d × (1 − Tc))
Cost of equity (CAPM):  Rₑ = Risk-free + β × (Equity Risk Premium)
Cost of debt:  yield / effective rate, × (1 − tax) for the shield

Worked: 70% equity / 30% debt; risk-free 4%, β 1.1, ERP 5%, cost of debt 6%, tax 25%. Cost of equity = 4% + 1.1×5% = 9.5%. After-tax cost of debt = 6%×(1−0.25) = 4.5%. WACC = 0.70×9.5% + 0.30×4.5% = 8.0%. The (1−tax) on debt is where the interest tax shield is counted (the reason NOPAT ignored it). Discipline: use MARKET-value weights; it's an estimate wearing a lab coat (ERP debated, β unstable) — hold it as a RANGE, not a decimal. Buffett rejects β ("volatility ≠ risk") and uses opportunity cost / the long-bond as his hurdle. Compare ROIC vs WACC: above = creates value, below = destroys it.

6 · The Leverage Cascade — DOL, DFL, DTL ↑ top

Setup: Revenue 100, variable costs 50, fixed operating costs 30, interest 10 → EBIT 20, pre-tax profit 10. Now revenue +10% → 110:

Variable → 55, fixed stays 30 → new EBIT = 25  (+25%)  → new profit = 25 − 10 = 15  (+50%)
DOL = %ΔEBIT ÷ %ΔRev = 25/10 = 2.5  (fixed OPERATING costs amplify)
DFL = %ΔNI ÷ %ΔEBIT = 50/25 = 2.0  (fixed FINANCING/debt amplifies)
DTL = DOL × DFL = 5.0 → every 1% of revenue swings NI by 5%

The recession gauge — and it's symmetric. Same firm: revenue −10% → NI −50%. High DOL (heavy fixed costs) + high DFL (heavy debt) = huge DTL = an earnings volatility machine. High-DTL firms are the recession PREY; low-DOL (variable cost) + low-debt firms are the survivors/predators. DOL is the operating cousin of your DSCR fragility; DFL is the financing cousin.

7 · Turnover ↔ Days — the 365 flip ↑ top

Days = 365 ÷ Turnover  |  Turnover = 365 ÷ Days

Turnover = how many times per year a pool is collected/replaced; Days = duration of one cycle. A/R turnover 12× → 365÷12 = 30 days customers take to pay. Same fact, two forms (frequency vs duration). Works for all: DIO (Days Inventory Outstanding = Inventory×365÷COGS, stock sitting), DSO (Days Sales Outstanding = Receivables×365÷Revenue, collection time), DPO (Days Payable Outstanding = Payables×365÷COGS, how slow you pay). Assemble: Cash Conversion Cycle = DIO + DSO − DPO = days cash is trapped; negative (Walmart) = suppliers/customers finance you = float/moat.

8 · Change in NWC, Accruals & Cash vs FCF ↑ top

Change in NWC = NWC(this year) − NWC(last year)  — the movement between two snapshots
Accruals = Net Income − Operating Cash Flow  — the paper-vs-cash gap

Why ΔNWC is in owner earnings/FCF: net income is accrual-based; ΔNWC corrects it to cash. An INCREASE in NWC (more inventory/receivables) CONSUMES cash → subtract. A DECREASE releases cash → add. Growth ties up working capital, so fast growers subtract a large change (the cash cost of growth). Accruals as a quality tell: HIGH accruals (profit not turning to cash) = LOW earnings quality — a red flag. Cash vs FCF: Cash is a STOCK (money on hand, one day, balance sheet); FCF is a FLOW (cash generated over a period, CFO − CapEx). FCF feeds the cash balance; a big pile ≠ strong FCF. Value the FLOW (DCF); read the STOCK for balance-sheet strength / dry powder.

ACCOUNTING FUNDAMENTALS · the bookkeeping under the statements

The mechanics beneath every ratio — how the books are kept, how the balance sheet is built, the debt vocabulary, and the adjusting entries where earnings quality is made (or faked).

Double-Entry, Debits & Credits ↑ top

ConceptWhat it is
Debit (DR) & Credit (CR)DR = LEFT side, CR = RIGHT side of an account — NOT good/bad or plus/minus. Names come from Latin: DR = Debtor (debere, "to owe"), CR = Creditor (credere) — first letter + last letter.
Double-Entry BookkeepingEvery transaction is recorded twice — one debit, one equal credit — so the books always balance and Assets = Liabilities + Equity holds. This identity is WHY every reconciliation in this doc works (Invested Capital = Net Assets, etc.).
The increase rules — "DEALER"DEA increase with a DEBIT: Dividends, Expenses, Assets (debit-normal). LER increase with a CREDIT: Liabilities, Equity, Revenue (credit-normal). Assets up = debit; liabilities/equity up = credit.
Why Equity is "CR & DR"Equity holds BOTH builders (Revenue, Share Capital = credit, increase equity) and drainers (Expenses, Dividends = debit, decrease equity). Expenses/dividends are "equity accounts" in the LOGIC (they drain equity) but appear on the income statement, not the balance-sheet equity section.
Worked example€100 cash sale: Debit Cash 100 (asset up) / Credit Revenue 100 (equity up via revenue). Buy €500 inventory on credit: Debit Inventory 500 / Credit Accounts Payable 500. Both balance — the equation stays true.
◆ Value-Investor LensYou don't do bookkeeping — but knowing that every statement figure is the sum of balanced double entries is what lets you trust the statements' internal consistency, and — more valuable — spot when something DOESN'T reconcile. A mismatch between the balance-sheet identity and the reported subtotals is often the first thread you pull to uncover aggressive accounting. Double-entry is the accounting version of conservation of energy; when the energy doesn't balance, someone's hiding something.

Balance Sheet Mechanics ↑ top

ConceptWhat it is
OrderingAssets by liquidity (Cash → Receivables → Inventory → PP&E — most-liquid first under US GAAP; IFRS/continental often reverse it). Liabilities by maturity (soonest-due first: payables → then long-term debt). Order by TIME in both columns.
US vs UK vs French formatsSame identity, arranged 3 ways. US: most-liquid listing, stops at Total Assets = Total Liab + Equity. UK ("Net Assets"): rearranges to A − L = E — computes Net Current Assets (working capital) & Net Assets (= Equity) on the page — best for analysis. French (bilan): least-liquid-first assets, financing ordered by permanence (Equity on top, then LT debt, then current).
Book Value= Total Assets − Total Liabilities = Shareholders' Equity — the accounting net worth (the anchor of P/B). HISTORICAL-cost based → can understate (internally-built brands, old land) or overstate (obsolete inventory). BVPS = Equity ÷ Shares.
Net Current Assets = Working CapitalSame formula, two names: Current Assets − Current Liabilities. "Net Current Assets" (UK/accounting) = "Working Capital" (finance). Operating/net WC refines it by excluding cash & short-term debt.
Operating vs Current Liabilities (≠ same)Different axes. Current = by MATURITY (<1yr — includes short-term DEBT like the revolver). Operating = by NATURE (from operations, non-interest-bearing — payables, accruals — EXCLUDES debt, can be long-term). Overlap but diverge — which is exactly why Invested Capital strips operating liabilities but keeps financing debt in.
Two "Net Assets" (recap)Accounting net assets = Total Assets − ALL liabilities = Equity (UK sheet's figure). Operating net assets = PP&E + Net Working Capital = Invested Capital (keeps debt in). Differ by the debt. Always ask "net of WHAT?"
◆ Value-Investor LensBook value is the accountant's value, not the market's — historical-cost based, so it hides the real worth of internally-built brands/IP (why asset-light compounders trade far above book, not "expensive") and can overstate stale assets. A stock below book (P/B < 1) is a bargain OR a value trap — the market may be right that the assets are worth less. Book value is most trustworthy for financials & asset-heavy firms (assets near market), least for asset-light ones. And the operating-vs-current-liabilities gap (short-term debt) is exactly the piece that decides whether your ROIC denominator is right — the single most common denominator error. Master "net of WHAT, sorted by which axis?" and no balance sheet in any country's format can fool you.

Debt & Liabilities Taxonomy ↑ top

TermWhat it is / distinction
The vocabulary hierarchyDebt = umbrella (any obligation to repay). Promissory note = the signed IOU instrument. Notes payable = the balance-sheet liability it creates. Loan = the borrowing transaction (usually documented by a note → shows as notes payable). Bonds payable = the capital-markets cousin (many tradable holders vs one lender).
Classes of notes payableBy maturity (short-term/current vs long-term); by interest (interest-bearing vs discount/zero-coupon); by security (secured/collateral vs unsecured); by repayment (bullet/term vs amortizing/installment); by source (bank, trade, related-party). = the debt-structuring dials you already turn in project finance.
Notes Payable vs Accounts PayableNotes Payable = formal, written, usually interest-bearing debt → counts in NET DEBT / invested capital. Accounts Payable = informal, non-interest-bearing trade credit (free supplier financing) → NOT debt. The key line for your leverage analysis.
Bank debt: principal vs interestPrincipal (outstanding balance) = a LIABILITY on the balance sheet; repaying it just reduces Cash & Notes Payable — NOT an expense. Interest = an INCOME-STATEMENT expense → reduces net income → flows into retained earnings (equity) at year-end — so it hits equity only INDIRECTLY. A loan payment splits: interest → P&L; principal → reduces the liability. (= your DSCR debt-service waterfall.)
◆ Value-Investor LensThe debt footnote is where the real leverage risk lives — the maturity schedule, secured vs unsecured, fixed vs floating, covenants. That's the DTL / net-debt-to-EBITDA story that decides recession survival (predator vs prey). Distinguish cash-settled liabilities (real burdens) from the free-financing kind (accounts payable). And your project-finance background is a genuine edge here: most equity analysts learned these as abstract categories, but you've negotiated every dial — secured/unsecured, bullet/amortizing, tenor, covenants — in real term sheets. You read a debt footnote faster than someone who's never sat at a lending table.

Adjusting Entries & Accruals ↑ top

Period-end entries that enforce accrual accounting — recognize revenue when EARNED and expense when INCURRED, regardless of cash. Two families × two sides:

TypeFamilyWhat it is / entry
Prepaid ExpenseDeferral (cash first)Paid cash up front → an ASSET (future benefit); expensed over time as consumed. Pay €1,200 insurance → monthly: Debit Insurance Expense 100 / Credit Prepaid Insurance 100.
Unearned RevenueDeferral (cash first)Received cash up front → a LIABILITY (you owe the service); recognized as revenue as delivered. Monthly: Debit Unearned Revenue 100 / Credit Service Revenue 100.
Accrued ExpenseAccrual (cash later)Incurred but NOT yet paid, NO invoice yet → a LIABILITY; often an ESTIMATE (wages, interest, utilities). Dec 31: Debit Wages Expense 5,000 / Credit Wages Payable 5,000.
Accrued RevenueAccrual (cash later)Earned but NOT yet billed → an ASSET (unbilled receivable); recognized early. Dec 31: Debit Accrued Receivable 3,000 / Credit Service Revenue 3,000.
DistinctionWhat it is
Deferrals vs AccrualsDeferrals = cash moved FIRST, recognition later (mechanical, low judgment). Accruals = recognition FIRST, cash later (estimates, HIGH judgment → manipulation surface).
Accrued Revenue vs Accounts ReceivableBoth assets (money in). AR = earned AND invoiced (documented claim). Accrued revenue = earned but NOT yet invoiced (estimated, converts to AR once billed). Invoice is the dividing line.
Accrued Expense vs Accounts PayableBoth liabilities (money out). AP = incurred WITH invoice received (exact amount). Accrued expense = incurred but NO invoice yet (often an estimate, converts to AP once billed).
Why accruals = earnings qualityAccruals involve ESTIMATES management controls → aggressive accrued revenue or under-accrued expenses flatter profit. Analytically Accruals = Net Income − Operating Cash Flow; high accruals = low earnings quality (Sloan anomaly — they reverse). The cash flow statement strips accruals out — why "cash is fact, earnings are opinion."
Unearned Revenue is FLOAT (worked)Airline sells a €1,000 ticket for a flight in 3 months: Debit Cash 1,000 / Credit Unearned Revenue 1,000 (owes a flight, a liability — NOT accounts payable, which is cash YOU owe a supplier; NOT revenue, not earned till flown). When flown: Debit Unearned Revenue / Credit Revenue. The deciding question on the liability side is "delivered yet?", not "documented?"
◆ Value-Investor LensUnearned revenue is one of the BEST liabilities a business can have — it's float. The company is paid in cash today for a service delivered later — holding the customer's money interest-free in between (airlines, annual-billed SaaS, insurers, gyms, Costco memberships). A large & growing unearned-revenue balance is a sign of pricing power and a customer-funded business model — the same negative-working-capital / float moat as Walmart's payables or Buffett's insurance float. It's a liability in name (you owe the service) but an asset in economics (you hold the cash first). Distinguish cash-settled liabilities (real burdens) from service-settled ones like unearned revenue (often hidden strengths) — the airline discharges its "debt" simply by flying a plane it was going to fly anyway.

And on accruals as the lie-detector: documented AR/AP are trustworthy; the ESTIMATED accrued items are the manipulation surface. Watch net income vs operating cash flow over several years — if profit rises while cash lags (accruals piling up), open the footnotes. AR/AP tell the working-capital/bargaining-power story; the accrued items tell the earnings-QUALITY story. Keep Munger's skepticism sharpest on the estimates.
